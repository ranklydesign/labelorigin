How do you sell skincare in your spa without being pushy?

Prescribe products as aftercare in the treatment room, not at checkout. Name the products during the treatment, record the recommendation on the client file, and send a reorder reminder before the product runs out — the Prescribe, record, replenish loop. Retail is roughly 15 to 40 percent of med-spa revenue when the loop runs consistently.

The loop is the whole method, and it works because it changes what the recommendation is. A product named in the treatment room, while the practitioner is explaining what was done and what the skin needs next, is professional advice. The same product named at the register, after the client has already paid, is a sales pitch — and clients know the difference instantly. The rest of this guide is about running the loop: when to recommend, how to train staff to do it, what the shelf is for, and how to tell whether it is working.

Retail is care, not an afterthought. The spas that retail well treat the product recommendation as part of the treatment plan — written on the aftercare form, recorded on the client file, followed up before the product runs out. The shelf is where the client collects it, not where the selling happens.

The loop: prescribe, record, replenish

Pabau, a med-spa practice platform, describes the loop that the practices that retail well all run: prescribe, record, replenish. The practitioner names the products in the treatment room as aftercare for the treatment just done. The purchase goes on the client file, next to the treatment notes, so the next practitioner sees the routine. A reorder reminder goes out before the product runs out. Run consistently, that loop can make retail a meaningful line in the accounts — published benchmarks put retail at roughly 15 to 40 percent of med-spa revenue, depending on the source.

The economics explain why the loop matters more than the shelf. Retail adds revenue without adding treatment-room hours: a product sale takes minutes, not a booked hour. And repeat buyers carry the category — Skytale Group found that 17 percent of skincare buyers generate nearly half of skincare revenue. The client who reorders a serum every eight weeks is worth more than ten clients who buy once and never return, which is why the third step of the loop, the reminder, is the one that makes the money.

The three windows

There are three moments when a recommendation lands naturally, and one when it does not. The first window is the consultation: while the client fills out the intake form or talks through their concerns, you are gathering the information the recommendation will be built on. What does their current nighttime routine look like? What have they tried? The second window is during the service itself — the client is relaxed, their eyes are closed, and you are using the product on them. Mentioning the take-home size of the lotion you are massaging in right now is a recommendation, not a pitch. The third window is the aftercare recap: explain what to use at home, in what order, and why.

The moment that is not a window is the register. Nothing kills the post-facial calm faster than being asked, as the client is paying, whether they want to buy something. The framing that works in the treatment room is the one Lip Gloss & Aftershave describes from its own practice: repeat back what the client said, name the concern you noticed, and recommend as a professional — "I heard you say your skin has been feeling dry and irritated, and I noticed some redness around your cheeks. My recommendation would be a series of calming treatments, and at home it is crucial to use the right products to reinforce them. I recommend starting with this one, because it calms and hydrates without feeling heavy." The product is the answer to a problem the client just described, not an item on a shelf.

Match the product to the treatment, not to the shelf. After a peel, recommend a calming serum and sun protection. After a facial, the cleanser and moisturizer you used on them. The client buys what you used on them — that is the entire logic of a house line, and it is why the start-a-line guide for med spas says to build the range around the treatment menu.

Staff who recommend are staff who believe

No script survives contact with a provider who does not believe in the product. The spas that retail well make product knowledge part of the culture: at each staff meeting, one to three team members share their current favorite product and why; new hires learn by matching products to skin concerns, sometimes gamified with trivia or flashcard drills; brand representatives run regular product-knowledge classes with seasonal recommendations and ingredient highlights. The detail that matters most: staff get full-size products to use themselves, or buy them at cost. When someone has firsthand experience with a product's texture, absorption, and how it layers, they speak about it with clarity and conviction — and if they do not believe in what they are recommending, neither will the client.

Training should be about listening, not closing. Train staff to listen first, then recommend based on the client's goals — and to use scripts that frame upgrades as personalized enhancements rather than add-ons. "To support your peel, we recommend this antioxidant serum for home use" is a recommendation tied to a result; "Would you like to add a serum?" is a question the client has to do the work of answering. WellnessLiving's retailing guide makes the same point from the other side: train staff in recommending the right products for the treatment, the client's age, and their concerns, and ask them what they would recommend in different scenarios rather than role-playing a pitch.

The shelf is the last place to start

The shelf matters — display products prominently, curate the retail space so it feels like part of the spa rather than a shop, and let clients buy online and pick up in person — but it is the last step, not the first. A beautiful shelf with no recommendation behind it is a display, not a revenue line. WellnessLiving's ten tips for spa retailing put the same order first: choose products that enhance the guest experience, curate the space, train the staff, and only then promote, reward loyalty, and track sales. Their figures: mastering spa retailing can lift revenue by 10 to 20 percent.

Two shelf mechanics are worth setting up early. The first is bundling: offer the regimen as a set, and loyalty points that reward the second and third purchase rather than the first — repeat buyers are the revenue, so reward the repeat. The second is the reorder reminder itself: a message timed to the product's expected life — six to eight weeks for a serum, for example — sent before the client runs out, while the recommendation is still fresh. The retail shelf ROI calculator shows how sell-through and reorder timing change the return on a shelf, and the margin calculator covers the math behind bundles and discounts.

Measure it or it drifts

The loop only works if every step happens every time, and the only way to know is to measure it. Three numbers are enough. Attach rate: what share of treatments produce a product sale — start by tracking it for your three highest-volume treatments, writing the products onto their aftercare forms. Repeat purchase rate: what share of buyers come back for the same product within its expected life. And retail share of revenue: the 15 to 40 percent benchmark, tracked monthly, so a drift shows up before it becomes a habit.

Pick the three highest-volume treatments this month, write their products onto the aftercare forms, and start tracking attach rate. That is the whole first step — no new shelf, no new products, no new script. Frequently Asked Questions

Selling skincare in your spa
How do you sell skincare without being pushy?

Prescribe products as aftercare for the treatment the client just had, in the treatment room — not as a pitch at checkout. Frame the recommendation around the client's own goals, name the product and why it fits, and let the client decide. Selling becomes recommending when the product supports the result they just paid for.

When is the best time to recommend products?

There are three windows: the consultation, when you learn the client's concerns; during the service, when the client is relaxed and you are using the product on them; and the aftercare recap, when you explain what to use at home. The register is not a window — ambushing a client at checkout is the fastest way to make retail feel like pressure.

How much revenue should retail bring a med spa?

Published benchmarks put retail at roughly 15 to 40 percent of med-spa revenue, depending on the source (Pabau, checked 31 October 2026). WellnessLiving puts the lift from good spa retailing at 10 to 20 percent of revenue. Retail adds revenue without adding treatment-room hours, and repeat product buyers keep spending between appointments.

How do I get my staff to recommend products?

Make product knowledge part of the culture, not a script. Share one favorite product per staff meeting, gamify matching products to skin concerns, and let staff use the products themselves — at cost if you can. A provider who has used the product speaks about it with conviction; one who has not will not recommend it.

Should I discount retail products to sell more?

Discount bundles and regimens rather than single bestsellers, and never discount below your true cost per unit. A 10 percent discount on a three-product regimen moves three units in one sale and introduces the full routine. Discounting a single product to cost teaches clients to wait for the next discount — see the pricing guide for the margin math.
