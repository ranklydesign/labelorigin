Freight and Warehousing Costs Explained

A product that has been made, filled, labelled, and packed still has to travel to you, and then it has to live somewhere until it is sold. Both steps cost money, both are easy to leave out of a first budget, and both are hard to absorb gracefully once a shipment is already on its way.

Why freight arrives as its own line

Freight is the cost of moving finished goods from the manufacturer to you, and it arrives as its own line because it is a different service, provided by different people, under different conditions from manufacturing itself. A manufacturer quote may include freight, exclude it, or offer it as a separate arrangement, and the difference matters a great deal to the total you will actually hand over. Founders who assume freight is included, without checking, can find that a production total they judged affordable becomes a delivered total they cannot comfortably meet. Founders who assume freight is excluded, without checking, can overstate the total and reject a workable quote. The only safe approach is to ask, in writing, whether freight is included, what delivery point it covers, and what would be charged separately. That question belongs in every quote comparison, alongside the production buckets, so that like is compared with like.

The published estimate for freight is $100–$1,000+ per shipment, an estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote. The range is wide because shipments differ widely: a small, light consignment travelling a short distance sits at a different point from a heavy palletised load travelling across the country. Treat the range as a starting point for judging quotes, not as a price from any carrier or manufacturer. When a freight quote arrives, compare it with the range and ask what explains its position. Distance, weight, shipment size, and handling requirements each move the charge, and a quote that explains those drivers is easier to trust than a single unexplained total. A quote that will not explain its drivers, or that leaves the delivery point unclear, deserves the same caution as a production quote that will not itemise its buckets.

What freight pricing reflects

Freight pricing reflects the work of moving goods safely from one place to another, and the main drivers are straightforward once they are named. Weight matters, because finished skincare goods are heavy: liquids in glass or thick plastic, packed in cartons, add up quickly, and a shipment that looks modest on paper can be surprisingly heavy on a scale. Distance matters, because a longer journey needs more fuel, more driver time, and more handling. Shipment size and shape matter, because a palletised load, a part load, and a small parcel are handled differently, priced differently, and suited to different kinds of shipment. Handling requirements matter too: goods that need careful handling, protection from damage, or particular loading arrangements cost more to move than goods that can be handled routinely. None of these drivers is mysterious, but together they explain why two founders shipping similar-looking orders can receive very different freight quotes.

Storage from the day the shipment arrives

Storage begins the day the shipment arrives, not the day sales begin, and that gap is where warehousing costs quietly accumulate. From arrival onward, goods need a clean, dry, organised place where each product and variant can be found, counted, and picked without confusion. They need to be kept away from damage, from extreme conditions, and from the everyday clutter that turns stock-keeping into guesswork. They need a system, however simple, for knowing what arrived, what has left, and what remains, so that reordering decisions rest on counts rather than impressions. That system can be a spreadsheet and labelled shelves, and it does not need to be elaborate to be effective. What it needs is consistency: every arrival recorded, every dispatch recorded, and regular checks that the record matches the shelves. A founder who knows the stock position can reorder calmly. A founder who does not know it will either reorder too early, tying up funds, or too late, running out at the wrong moment.

The cost of storage is not only rent. It includes the time spent receiving, checking, organising, and counting stock, the materials used to keep it organised, and the cost of any damage or loss that poor organisation allows. It also includes the opportunity cost of space used for stock that is not moving: space taken by slow-moving variants is space unavailable for faster-moving products, and funds tied up in unsold stock are funds unavailable for the next run or for reaching buyers. Storage decisions should therefore be tied to run sizing. A run sized to realistic sell-through, with variants kept to a manageable number, is easier and cheaper to store than a large, sprawling run ordered on optimism. Smaller, better-organised stock is not only cheaper to keep. It is easier to count, easier to pick, easier to present to buyers, and easier to learn from, because the sales pattern of a focused range is clearer than the pattern of a range that tries to cover everything at once.

Why a freight quote belongs early in planning

A freight quote belongs early in planning because it changes decisions that are expensive to change later. Run size, packaging, delivery address, and timing can all be adjusted while the plan is still on paper. Once production has been ordered, those choices are largely fixed, and freight becomes a charge to be absorbed rather than a cost to be planned. An early quote lets the founder judge the full delivered cost while the plan is still flexible, compare manufacturers on a like-for-like delivered basis, and spot a freight surprise before it becomes a budget problem. It also lets the founder test assumptions: whether a distant manufacturer remains competitive once freight is included, whether a heavier pack is worth its freight consequence, and whether splitting a shipment would help storage and cash flow enough to justify its extra handling. Early information does not remove cost. It moves the cost into the planning stage, where it can shape decisions, instead of leaving it to the delivery stage, where it can only be met.

Get the freight quote before you fix the run. Distance, weight, and shipment size can move the delivered total enough to change the run size, the packaging, or the manufacturer choice. A quote obtained early is planning information. A quote obtained late is a bill.

Reading a freight quote

Reading a freight quote means checking what it covers, what it excludes, and what could change it. Start with the delivery point: does the quote cover delivery to your address, to a depot, or to some other point, and what happens after that point? Check whether loading, unloading, and handling are included, and what is expected of you on arrival. Check the timing: when the goods will leave, when they are expected to arrive, and what happens if timing slips. Check the basis of the charge: weight, size, distance, or a combination, and whether the charge would change if the actual weight or size differs from the estimate. Check what is excluded, such as waiting time, redelivery, special handling, or storage if delivery cannot be completed. And check the position on damage: how damage should be noted on arrival, what evidence is needed, and what time limits apply. A quote that answers those points plainly can be compared and relied on. A quote that leaves them vague should be clarified before it is accepted, not after the goods have moved.

  • Delivery point. Where the quote delivers to, and what happens beyond that point.
  • Inclusions and exclusions. Loading, unloading, handling, waiting time, and redelivery, each stated plainly.
  • Basis of charge. Weight, size, distance, and what happens if the actual shipment differs from the estimate.
  • Timing. Departure, expected arrival, and the position if timing slips.
  • Damage and shortage. How to note problems on arrival, what evidence is needed, and what time limits apply.

Planning freight alongside production

Freight planning belongs alongside production planning, in the same worksheet and the same review rhythm. Give freight its own line, with the published estimate as a starting point and the actual quote entered as soon as it arrives. The published estimate for freight is $100–$1,000+ per shipment, an estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote, and the line should be updated with real quotes rather than left at the estimate once planning firms up. Review the freight line whenever production plans change, because a change in run size, packaging, or delivery address can move freight even when production pricing barely moves. When you read freight and shipping costs in the skincare line cost guide, you will see freight sitting as its own row in the cost worksheet, beside development, fill, packaging, labels, testing, samples, and setup. That placement is deliberate: freight is a bucket in its own right, to be quoted and judged like any other, not an afterthought to be absorbed when the goods arrive.
