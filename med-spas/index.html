Private-label skincare for med spas

Med-spa clients arrive already educated — they have read the ingredient lists and they ask hard questions at the consultation. A private-label line lets you answer with products that carry your name and your protocols, sold as the aftercare to the treatments you already perform. This page covers how to build that line honestly, what to demand from a manufacturer, and why small runs fit the way med spas actually sell.

What a med-spa line looks like

A med-spa line is usually built around the treatment menu: a gentle cleanser and barrier moisturizer for post-procedure care, a daily serum for maintenance between visits, sun protection, and one or two targeted products matched to your most-booked services. The products are sold as aftercare first and retail second — the consultation recommends them, the front desk dispenses them, and the client returns because the routine works. That aftercare framing is what separates a med-spa line from a generic retail shelf: every product has a protocol reason to exist.

Because your clients are already ingredient-literate, the line lives or dies on transparency. Full ingredient lists, plain explanations of what each product does, and no borrowed clinical language you cannot support. Clients who trust the clinic trust the products; clients who catch an overstatement stop trusting both.

Positioning that stays honest

The temptation in this segment is to let the marketing outrun the evidence — borrowed before-and-after language, implied treatment claims, words like "clinical" doing heavy lifting. Resist it. The durable positioning is simpler: products formulated for use alongside your treatments, selected by your providers, sold with real ingredient lists and realistic expectations. That is already a strong claim, and it is one you can defend in a consultation. If a manufacturer or a label draft offers you claim language you would not say to a client face to face, do not put it on a label.

Your providers are the quality filter. If the clinicians performing the treatments would not recommend a product to their own patients, it does not belong in your line — no matter how good the margin looks on paper.

The five steps, in general terms

Every manufacturer runs its own version of this process, but the shape is consistent across the industry. Knowing the steps in advance lets you spot where a quote ends and where extra work — and extra cost — begins.

  1. Choose your path. Stock (private-label) formulas that already exist, or a custom formula developed to your specification. Stock is faster and cheaper to start; custom gives you a formula nobody else sells. Our guide to private label vs. custom formulation compares the two paths in detail.
  2. Evaluate samples. Test the product the way your clients will use it — on skin, over days, in your treatment room or at home. One sample round rarely settles it; budget time for a second round on anything custom.
  3. Branding and packaging. Your label, your container, your outer packaging. The brand owner is generally responsible for label content and regulatory compliance, so confirm early who supplies the artwork, who checks the ingredient list, and what the label minimums are.
  4. Testing and compliance checks. Stability, preservative-effectiveness, and safety testing expectations depend on the product type and where you sell. Ask what testing is included in the quote and what is billed separately — and read our MoCRA explainer for the brand-owner responsibilities that sit with you either way.
  5. First production run and reorder. Production runs to an agreed quantity, ships to you, and the relationship continues into reorders — which usually carry the same minimums, so plan the second run before the first sells out.

What to ask a manufacturer

  • Full ingredient disclosure: complete lists for every formula, including fragrance components, before you approve anything.
  • Testing included vs. billed separately: stability, preservative-effectiveness, and any safety testing, itemized in the quote.
  • Post-procedure suitability: which stock formulas are designed for sensitive or recovering skin, and what evidence supports that.
  • Claim support: what the manufacturer will and will not put in writing about a product — and a refusal to support a claim is useful information.
  • Minimums and reorders: per-SKU numbers, and whether the same minimums apply to every reorder.
  • Label review: who checks the label, and whether regulatory review is part of the service or an extra.

A sensible starter lineup

Start from your top three services and work backward: the aftercare each one needs, the maintenance product the client uses between visits, and the daily staple (cleanser, moisturizer, sun protection) that every client needs regardless of treatment. That usually lands at four to six products — enough to cover the protocols, short enough to manage. Add targeted products only when a service line justifies them with real volume.

Frequently Asked Questions

Private label for med spas
Can a med spa sell its own branded skincare?

Yes — private-label programs exist for exactly this, and the aftercare framing fits med spas well. The products are your brand, made by a manufacturer to specification or selected from stock formulas. Labeling and regulatory obligations generally sit with you as the brand owner, so confirm the label review process before launch.

What claims can a med-spa line make?

Only claims the product and its evidence support. Avoid borrowed clinical language and treatment claims you cannot defend in a consultation. Ask any manufacturer what claim language they will support in writing — and treat an unwillingness to put a claim in writing as a signal.

Do I need separate products for post-procedure care?

Often yes: skin recovering from a procedure needs gentler, simpler formulas than daily maintenance products. Ask which stock formulas are suited to sensitive or recovering skin, and have your providers evaluate samples on that basis specifically.

How much stock should a med spa hold?

Size the first run to a few months of realistic sales across your client base, not to the manufacturer's largest tier. Reorder minimums usually match first-order minimums, so a short range with planned reorders beats a large one-off order that expires on the shelf.
