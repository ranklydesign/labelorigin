Payment Terms and the Cash-Flow Gap

The most uncomfortable moment in a first launch is rarely a problem with the product. It is the moment a founder realises that cash has been committed, in stages, well before any revenue has arrived, and that the gap between committing cash and making a first sale is longer, and more demanding, than the budget suggested.

When money actually leaves

A launch budget is usually read as a single total, and that reading hides the question that matters most for cash flow: when does each part of the total actually leave the founder's hands? Production is not a single handover of funds on a single day. It is a sequence of stages, each with its own trigger, its own timing, and its own demand on cash. Development work, where it applies, is committed before production begins. Materials are ordered, production is scheduled, and goods are filled, labelled, and packed, each step consuming time and, in many arrangements, funds. Freight is committed around delivery. Storage begins on arrival. Launch effort, from content to outreach, runs alongside all of it, in steady amounts rather than in a single step. A founder who knows only the total, without knowing the stages, cannot tell whether funds will be available at each stage, or whether a stage will fall due before revenue from earlier work has arrived to cover it.

Deposit and balance practice

As a general observation — not a fixed term, and not a promise from any manufacturer — manufacturers commonly require a deposit of around half, with the balance due before shipment. That observation, from the cost guide, describes a common pattern, not a rule that every manufacturer follows, and it should never be treated as the term a particular manufacturer will offer. Some manufacturers may ask for a different proportion, a different staging, or different triggers, and the only terms that matter are the terms a particular manufacturer puts in writing for a particular order. The value of the observation is that it gives founders a realistic starting expectation: a substantial share of the production cost is commonly committed up front, to start the work, and the remainder is commonly required before goods are released for shipment. Planning on that shape, while confirming the actual terms in writing, is far safer than planning as if the full amount were due only on delivery, or as if a small holding sum would suffice to start production.

The balance stage deserves equal attention, because it is the point at which goods that are ready still cannot move. A balance due before shipment means that the founder must have the remaining funds available, in full, at the moment shipment is ready, regardless of whether sales have begun, whether the launch effort has borne fruit, or whether other costs have arisen in the meantime. That timing can catch founders who assumed the balance could be covered from early sales, because early sales cannot exist until the goods have shipped, arrived, and been offered to buyers. The balance must therefore be funded from reserves, from revenue earned before the launch, or from other planned sources, and the plan for funding it should be settled before the deposit is sent. A founder who sends a deposit without a plan for the balance has funded the beginning of the launch without funding its completion, and the goods may sit, ready but unshipped, while the balance is gathered under pressure.

The gap between cash committed and first sale

The cash-flow gap is the period between the first funds being committed and the first sale revenue arriving in a usable form. It begins when the deposit, or the first development or sampling charge, is handed over, and it ends when sales revenue has arrived, cleared, and become available to cover further costs. Between those points lie production, delivery, storage, listing, presentation, outreach, and the time buyers need to discover, consider, and choose the product. Each step takes time, and the steps run in sequence, so the gap is commonly measured in weeks or months rather than days. That length is normal, and it is not a sign that anything has gone wrong. It is the natural shape of a physical product launch, and it must be planned for as a normal feature, not treated as an unexpected delay to be endured.

Plan the gap as a budget line of its own. The period between committing cash and receiving revenue needs funds, time, and patience. Reserving for the gap before the first stage falls due turns waiting from a crisis into a planned part of the launch.

Planning the gap, not discovering it

Planning the gap starts with a full picture of the commitment, in stages, with timing. List each stage, from development and sampling through production, freight, storage, and launch effort, and note the trigger and timing for each one. Total the stages, but keep the staging visible, so the founder can see not only how much will be committed, but when. Then set the funds for the gap aside, in a clearly identified reserve, before the first stage falls due. The reserve should cover the stages themselves, plus the running costs of the gap period, plus a margin for slippage in timing or for costs that arrive earlier than expected. A reserve that covers only the stages, with no margin, will be exhausted by the first unexpected shift. A reserve with a margin can absorb a delay in sampling, a shift in delivery, or a slower start to sales without forcing a hurried decision.

  • Stages mapped. Each stage listed with its trigger and timing, so the shape of the commitment is visible.
  • Reserve set aside. Funds for the stages, the gap period, and a margin for slippage, identified before the first stage falls due.
  • Launch effort funded. Content, outreach, and relationship work funded from the reserve, on a steady timetable, not from hoped-for early sales.
  • Sales assumed gradual. Revenue planned conservatively, so a slow start is absorbed calmly rather than treated as a crisis.
  • Regular review. The gap plan revisited as stages complete and sales begin, with the reserve and timetable adjusted on evidence.

Questions to ask before committing

Before committing funds, ask the manufacturer for the payment terms in full, in writing, and check that the answers cover every stage of the commitment. Ask what proportion is required as a deposit, what triggers the deposit, and what the deposit secures. Ask when the balance falls due, what triggers it, and whether shipment is conditional on the balance being received in full. Ask whether any other charges fall due at particular stages, such as development, testing, sampling, or freight, and when each one is expected. Ask what happens if a stage is delayed, whether by sampling, packaging, or production, and whether the payment timetable moves with the delay or stays fixed. And ask what happens if the specification changes after the deposit, whether through revision, variant changes, or packaging changes, and how any additional work would be charged and timed. Clear answers to those questions show how the relationship works after funds are committed, not just before, and they give the founder the information needed to plan the gap honestly.

Keeping payment terms in writing

Payment terms should be kept in writing, with the quote, as part of the order record. The written record should state the stages, the triggers, the timing, and the amounts or proportions that fall due at each stage, together with the position on delays, changes, and holding goods. It should state what is included in each stage and what is charged separately, so that no stage arrives with an unexpected addition. It should be dated, and any change to the terms should be recorded as a dated revision, agreed by both sides, rather than as a verbal understanding that may be remembered differently later. A written record protects both sides: the manufacturer can plan production and materials with confidence, and the founder can plan funds with confidence, because both are working from the same terms.

The gap between cash committed and first sale is not a flaw in the launch model. It is the launch model, for any physical product, and it rewards founders who plan for it openly. Map the stages, confirm the terms, reserve the funds, fund the launch effort steadily, and assume sales will build gradually. Do that work before the first stage falls due, and the gap becomes a planned period of preparation, during which the product is made, the audience is built, and the business is readied for selling. Skip that work, and the gap becomes a period of pressure, during which funds run thin, decisions are hurried, and the launch is managed from crisis to crisis. The difference is planning, and planning is available to every founder before a single sum is committed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Payment terms
When does my money leave under deposit and balance terms?

Cash is committed in stages, with a deposit to start production and a balance due before shipment. Get the stages, the triggers, and the timing in writing before you commit, so the gap to the first sale can be planned.

How do I plan for the gap before the first sale?

Plan the gap as its own budget line, with funds reserved for each stage before it falls due. Include freight, storage, and launch effort alongside production, and do not assume sales revenue will arrive in time to cover a stage.
