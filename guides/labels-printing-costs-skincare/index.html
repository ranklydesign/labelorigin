Labels and Printing Costs for a Launch

A label looks like a small part of a launch until its full cost is counted. This guide separates the printed label from application, cartons, inserts, and seals, so each line can be budgeted and compared on its own terms.

Updated 28 October 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges below as starting points for your own quotes.

What a label line item really covers

Founders often budget for labels as though the printed sticker were the whole cost. In practice, the label line on a quote can cover a chain of work: designing and preparing the artwork for print, printing the label itself, checking the printed result, applying the label to the container, and handling the finished unit through cartons, inserts, and seals.

The published band for labels in the cost guide is $0.10–$1.00 per label (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote). That band describes the printed label at small volumes, and it is deliberately wide because label printing varies with material, finish, size, quantity, and the number of colours and effects involved.

For the full set of launch buckets, see labels and printing costs in the skincare line cost guide. Labels sit alongside packaging in the cost guide, and the two lines are closely linked, because the label must fit the container, suit the closure, and sit comfortably within any outer carton.

Budget the chain, not just the sticker. Printing, application, cartons, inserts, and seals are separate lines with separate drivers, and a budget that names only the printed label will understate the labelling cost of a first launch.

As a general observation in the pillar guide, packaging and labels together are often 20–40% of per-unit cost on a small first run. That observation is a general observation, not a published band, and it is labelled here as such so it is not mistaken for a fixed share.

The printed label itself

The printed label is the face of the product on the shelf, and its cost is shaped by decisions that are easy to make for appearance alone. Material is the first of those decisions.

Size and shape follow from the container and from the information the label must carry. A label must fit the available surface of the container, leave room for the closure and for handling, and carry the required text legibly.

Printing method is the part of the label decision that founders see least, and it shapes the per-label figure more than any other single factor at small volumes. Digital printing suits smaller runs because it avoids some of the setup work involved in longer-run methods, and it allows a label design to be revised without discarding a large stock of printed labels. The published band of $0.10–$1.00 per label (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote) reflects this spread, and a quote should state the printing method and the quantity it assumes, so the per-label figure can be understood rather than merely compared.

Artwork preparation sits before printing and is sometimes overlooked in a label budget. The design must be prepared for print at the correct size and resolution, with colours specified for the printing method, text checked for accuracy, and any required information placed correctly.

Application and handling

Applying a label sounds simple, and at small volumes it often is done by hand, with each label placed, smoothed, and checked individually. Hand application suits a small first run because it needs no dedicated equipment and it allows each unit to be inspected as it is labelled.

Handling after application matters as well, because a freshly labelled unit must be packed without scuffing the label, stored without labels lifting or marking, and shipped without the outer packing damaging the finish. A label with a delicate finish needs more careful packing than a robust label, and a container with a curved surface needs the label to be applied with enough care that its edges stay down through handling and transit.

The distinction between labelling obligations and labelling costs is worth keeping clear. The companion guide on brand-owner duties covers what a brand owner is responsible for in labelling, and it is a useful companion to this guide, because it explains the responsibilities that sit with the brand owner. This guide covers the production cost lines only. For the responsibilities side, see brand-owner duties for skincare labelling. Keeping the two discussions separate prevents a common confusion in which a founder assumes that a manufacturer handling the printing is also handling the responsibility for the label content. Printing and responsibility are different matters, and both deserve their own attention before a launch.

Cartons, inserts, and seals

Outer cartons are a separate line from the label, even though they carry printed information and branding. A carton involves its own material, printing, cutting, folding, and assembly, and its cost is shaped by its size, its material, its print finish, and the quantity ordered.

Inserts are the leaflets or cards placed inside a carton or alongside a container, and they carry information, instructions, or brand storytelling that does not fit on the label itself. An insert involves its own printing, cutting, and folding, and its own quantity considerations, because inserts are usually ordered to match the run size and must be revised whenever the information they carry changes.

The interaction between these elements and the label is where the budget can grow quietly. A carton that carries the same branding as the label needs artwork prepared for a different format and size.

Why each variant needs its own run

Variants are the quiet multiplier in labelling costs. A variant is a version of a product that differs in scent, size, shade, or another detail, and each variant usually needs its own label because the printed information must match the product inside.

The effect compounds across the range. A launch with a small set of products, each offered in a small set of variants, can find itself managing a large number of label runs, each with its own quantity, its own artwork, and its own checking. The published band of $0.10–$1.00 per label (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote) applies per label, and the total labelling figure is the per-label figure multiplied across every variant and every unit.

Trimming variants is not a retreat from ambition. It is a way of keeping the first launch focused on the products and variants that can be sold through, while later variants are added with evidence from the first launch behind them. The general observation that packaging and labels together are often 20–40% of per-unit cost on a small first run is a general observation, not a published band, and variants are one of the main reasons that combined share can sit toward the higher end.

Proof checking deserves a final note, because it is the stage where a small error can become a printed run. A founder should read the proof slowly, word by word, checking the product name, the variant details, and every line of required text against the product specification, and should ask a second person to read it as well, since a fresh pair of eyes often catches what familiarity overlooks. Any correction should be marked clearly, a revised proof should be requested, and printing should not begin until the revised proof has been checked and approved in writing. That sequence takes time, and it should be allowed for in the launch schedule, so proof checking is never rushed to meet a printing date that was set before the artwork was ready.

Getting a label quote you can compare

A label quote is only useful if it can be compared with another quote on the same basis. The basis includes the label material and finish, the size and shape, the printing method, the quantity, the number of variants, and the lines for application, cartons, inserts, and seals.

  • Itemize every line. The printed label, application, cartons, inserts, and seals should appear separately, with the quantity and specification for each line stated plainly.
  • State the label specification. Material, finish, size, shape, and printing method should be written down, so two quotes describe the same label rather than two different labels at two different prices.
  • List every variant. Every product and every variant should appear by name, with the label run each one needs, so the variant multiplier is visible before printing.
  • Confirm the text and artwork check. Ask how proofs will be supplied, how revisions are handled, and what happens if an error is found after printing has begun.
  • Confirm application and packing. Ask whether application is included, how labelled units will be packed, and how the label finish will be protected through transit.
  • Ask about reorders. Ask whether the same specification, method, and lines will apply on a reorder, and what would change if the quantity or the variant list changes.

The practical conclusion is to treat labels as a production chain with a visible cost at each step, rather than as a single sticker cost. The printed label band of $0.10–$1.00 per label (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote) is the starting point, and application, cartons, inserts, seals, and variant runs are the lines that complete the picture.

Frequently Asked Questions

Labels and printing costs
Why does each variant need its own label run?

Each variant usually differs in scent, size, shade, or another detail that must appear on the label, so sharing one run across variants would leave the printed information wrong for at least one of them.

What should a label quote itemize?

A label quote should itemize the printed label, application, cartons, inserts, and seals separately, so you can see which lines change when a variant is added or a quantity changes.
