White label vs. private label skincare: what’s the difference?

The two terms are used interchangeably so often that the distinction has nearly disappeared — which is exactly why it matters. Private label and white label are different deals: different formula ownership, different minimums, different speed, and a different answer to the question your clients will eventually ask — is this product actually yours?

What is the difference between private label and white label skincare?

Private label means a manufacturer makes a product to your specification — your formula, or a stock formula adjusted for you — and only you sell it. White label means you buy a ready-made product and put your label on it; the same product may be sold under other brands. The difference is who owns the formula.

The confusion is honest: plenty of suppliers call everything “private label,” and plenty of buyers use “white label” to mean the same thing. The distinction this guide uses is the one that matters commercially. In private label, the product is made for you — either developed to your specification or a stock formula adjusted with your scent, actives, or packaging — and you are the only brand selling that version. In white label, the product already exists, the formula is shared, and your label is the only thing that makes it yours.

What white label skincare actually is

White label skincare is a finished product from a manufacturer’s existing catalog, sold to you with your brand on it. The formula already exists, the testing is already done, and the same product can be — and usually is — sold to other brands under their labels. Nothing is developed for you. What you are buying is speed, a formula that is already on the market, and the lowest commitment in the industry.

That is what a search for a white label skincare manufacturer is really looking for: a maker whose catalog you can brand. In practice it runs in four steps. You choose products from the maker’s stock range. You supply your label and packaging choices — some makers include label design in the program. You order at the maker’s published minimum for that tier. And you sell a product that was ready before you arrived. The maker’s name for the tier varies — white label, stock private label, ready-to-brand — which is exactly why the two terms in this guide’s title blur. The deal underneath is the same.

Who it fits: a first line, a short aftercare range matched to a treatment menu, back-bar products you go through quickly, and any situation where learning what your clients actually buy matters more than owning the formula. Who it does not fit: anyone whose pitch is “you can only get this from me.” With white label that sentence is not true — a competitor can order the identical product tomorrow, and the honest version of the white-label pitch is convenience and speed, not exclusivity.

The middle ground is real and common: stock private label. You take an existing formula, put your branding on it, and sometimes adjust scent or packaging. It behaves like white label on speed, minimums, and formula ownership — the formula stays the maker’s — and what is exclusively yours is the branded version you sell. Most first lines start here — it is the stock tier in our low-MOQ explainer, and the comparison with full custom formulation is covered in private label vs. custom formulation.

Who owns the formula?

This is the question to settle in writing before you pay anything, because it decides what happens when the relationship ends. In white label, the formula is the manufacturer’s, full stop — you are buying finished product, not a formula, and the same product can appear under another brand’s label. In private label, it depends on the tier. A fully custom formula developed to your specification is typically yours, and some manufacturers hand over the formula once it is created; others hold it until you reach a volume threshold. A stock private-label formula stays the manufacturer’s even though only you sell your branded version of it.

Ask three things, in writing: who owns the formula, what happens to it if you stop ordering, and whether you can take it to another manufacturer. A manufacturer that answers plainly is showing you how it thinks. One that will not answer is telling you something too.

What each model costs, in general terms

White label is usually the cheapest way in: no development work, a shared formula, and the lowest minimums, so the cash you commit before selling anything is small. Private label costs more at small runs, because development, setup, and testing are spread across fewer units — and the more custom the formula, the higher the minimum and the longer the timeline. That is the trade in one sentence: white label buys speed and a low commitment; private label buys exclusivity and control, at a higher commitment.

We do not quote prices on this page, because per-unit prices without a minimum, a packaging tier, and a quantity are meaningless. What we can point to is real: the published MOQ table lists 18 manufacturers’ published minimums with sources and dates, so you can see what each tier actually requires. For the cost side, our cost guide gives the first-run bands for stock and custom launches, and the margin calculator runs the arithmetic on your own numbers.

Compare the total commitment, not the per-unit price. A white-label unit that costs less but sits on the shelf is a worse buy than a private-label unit that costs more and sells. Units times all-in cost is the number your budget has to cover.

Which model fits a med spa or esthetician?

For a first line, white label or stock private label. Both are fast, both carry the lowest minimums, and both let you learn what your clients actually buy before you commit to development. A med spa’s aftercare products — cleanser, barrier moisturizer, serum, sun protection — are a natural white-label or stock private-label start, because the formulas are already on the market and the range is short. An esthetician’s first line is the same shape: the products you already use in every facial, under your own label.

Move to private label once you know which products sell and want a formula no competitor can buy. That is when exclusivity starts paying for itself: a client who can only get your serum from you is a client who comes back to you. Custom formulation is the last step, not the first — it is for the product you already know sells, now made exactly the way you want it.

The decision checklist

Work through these before you sign anything:

  • Is the formula shared with other brands, or made for me alone?
  • Who owns the formula, and what happens to it if I stop ordering?
  • What is the minimum per SKU — and how does the manufacturer define a SKU?
  • How fast is the first run, and what does the timeline assume (artwork approved, deposit paid)?
  • What testing and documentation come with the order, and what is billed separately?
  • Can I start white label or stock private label and move to custom later with the same manufacturer?
  • What are the reorder minimums, and do they match the first order?
  • Who is the responsible person for MoCRA purposes — me, or the manufacturer?

If you are choosing between manufacturers rather than models, our buyer checklist is the brief to send, and the US manufacturers list compares twelve of them on published minimums and specialties.

Frequently Asked Questions

Private label vs. white label skincare
Is white label the same as private label?

Not quite, though the terms are often used interchangeably. In the distinction this guide uses, private label means a product made to your specification — your formula, or a stock formula adjusted for you — that only you sell. White label means a ready-made product you put your label on, which the same manufacturer may sell under other brands too.

Which is cheaper, private label or white label?

White label is usually cheaper upfront: no development work, a shared formula, and the lowest minimums. Private label costs more at small runs, because development and setup are spread across fewer units. Compare the total commitment — units times all-in cost — not the per-unit price alone.

Can I switch from white label to private label later?

Yes — it is the most common path. Start white label or stock private label to learn what sells, then move to semi-custom or fully custom private label once sales justify the higher minimums and development time.
