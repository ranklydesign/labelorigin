Reading a manufacturer quote, line by line

A quote total tells you almost nothing on its own. This guide teaches you to read a quote row by row, using the eight worksheet rows from the cost guide, so you can see what is included, what is missing, and whether two quotes can fairly be compared.

Updated 28 October 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges below as starting points for your own quotes.

Start with the same basis

A manufacturer quote is an answer to a brief, and two answers to two different briefs cannot be compared by their totals. Before you look at any figure, check that every quote was written for the same products, the same units per product, and the same packaging. Same products means the same formulas and formats, not merely similar ones. Same units means the same count for each product, counted per product rather than across the brand as a whole. Same packaging means the same containers, closures, and outer cartons, or a clearly stated stock option on every quote. When any of those elements differ, the totals will differ for reasons that have nothing to do with value, and a founder who chooses on totals alone may choose the quote that simply left more out.

The worksheet enforces sameness. It is the eight-row check in the cost worksheet in the skincare line cost guide: copy the rows, get one figure per row from each manufacturer, and the total becomes a real comparison rather than a guess. A manufacturer who bundles several buckets into a single line can still be compared, but only once the bundle is unpacked into its rows. A manufacturer who omits a bucket can still be considered, but only once the omission is named and priced. Every range in this guide is an estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote. A figure outside a range is not automatically wrong; it may reflect a different product, pack, or scope. What matters is whether the manufacturer can explain the figure in terms of the row it sits in.

The worksheet, row by row

The table below sets out all eight worksheet rows with their published ranges. Use it as a checklist when a quote arrives: find each row, note what the quote says, and mark anything that is missing, bundled, or unclear. The explanations that follow take each row in turn, so you know what to look for and what to ask.

Formula development

This row applies only to custom formulation. The published range is $2,000–$15,000+ per formula, covering chemist time, raw materials for trial batches, and revision rounds. Read it for three things: is the fee stated per formula, is it billed separately from the production run, and is any part of it credited against the first order? A quote that states a single development figure for the whole launch, without saying how many formulas it covers, cannot be compared with a quote that prices development per formula. Ask for the per-formula figure and the credit position in writing, because both change the real cost of the custom path in ways a total never shows.

Product fill

Fill is the per-unit price of the product itself, multiplied by the units for that product. The published range is $1.50–$12.00 per unit, by product type. Check that the per-unit figure and the unit count are both stated, and that the multiplication is visible. A quote showing only a lump sum for fill, without the per-unit figure or the count behind it, cannot be checked. Also check that fill is counted per product: variants count separately, so a scent or size variant is a separate fill run with its own units and figure. That breakdown is what lets you see which product drives the total, and what would change if you dropped a variant or changed a unit count.

Packaging

Packaging covers jars, bottles, pumps, caps, droppers, tubes, and outer cartons, usually billed per unit on top of the fill price. The published range is $0.50–$5.00 per unit. Ask what the per-unit figure includes: the container alone, or the container plus its closure? An outer carton, or is the carton a separate line? Any assembly work, or is that charged elsewhere? Quotes often differ because they include different components, and a founder who does not unpack the row will compare a container-only figure with a container-plus-closure figure and draw the wrong conclusion. Note whether each component carries its own supplier minimum, because a component minimum can set the floor for a run even when the fill minimum would allow fewer units.

Labels and printing

The labels row is small in value and large in consequence. The published range is $0.10–$1.00 per label, covering the printed label itself, with application, outer cartons, inserts, and seals often sitting as separate lines. Read it for completeness rather than price alone: does it include the printed label, its application, and required regulatory text such as ingredient lists and net contents? Does it include outer cartons and inserts, or are those listed elsewhere — or nowhere? And does it price a separate label run for each variant? Every size, scent, or shade variant usually needs its own label run, so a quote pricing labels once for the whole launch is almost certainly incomplete, and an incomplete total leads to surprise costs after commitment.

Testing package

Testing is the row most often left vague, and the one where vagueness costs most. The published range is $500–$3,000 per formula, and the row should name the testing package, not merely a testing figure. Ask for the test list in writing: which items are included in the quoted figure, and which are billed separately? A quote stating a testing figure without a test list cannot be compared with one that itemises its tests, because the two figures may cover different scopes. Two quotes with similar testing figures may include very different work. Get the list, compare lists, and only then compare figures. If a manufacturer cannot provide the list, treat that as information about the relationship, not merely about the quote.

Freight and shipping

Freight covers getting finished goods to you, and it is the row founders most often discover late. The published range is $100–$1,000+ per shipment. Check whether freight is included at all, and if so, on what basis: does the figure cover shipment to your location, or only to a depot? Does it reflect the weight and bulk of the finished goods, including packaging and cartons, or is it a generic allowance? Finished goods are heavy, and a figure estimated before the pack was finalised may not survive contact with the real shipment. Get a freight quote early, on the basis of the actual products, units, and packaging, and treat any quote omitting freight entirely as incomplete rather than generous. Freight omitted is freight still to come.

What a missing row means

A missing row is information, not an accident to gloss over. It may mean the work is included in another row, in which case the quote should say so. It may mean the work is billed separately later, in which case the quote should say that too, with the basis for the later charge. Or it may mean the work has been overlooked, in which case the founder will meet the cost after committing, when their leverage is gone. The right response is the same in every case: ask which row covers the work, what is included and what is billed separately, and ask for the answer in writing. A manufacturer who answers clearly and promptly is showing how the relationship will work after you commit. One who answers vaguely, or treats the question as an irritation, is also showing how the relationship will work, and the founder should weigh that showing seriously.

Comparing quotes side by side

Once the rows are aligned, compare scope before price, row by row. For testing, compare test lists. For packaging, compare included components. For labels, compare whether cartons and inserts are in or out. For freight, compare destination and basis. Only when scope is aligned does price comparison mean anything: two figures for the same row, on the same scope, can be judged, while two figures for different scopes cannot, and the lower figure may simply be the narrower scope. Keep the worksheet after the comparison, whatever you decide. It becomes the record of what was quoted, on what basis, and with what scope, and if the scope changes later, it shows exactly which rows move. The guide to choosing a skincare manufacturer covers weighing vendors as a whole; this guide covers reading a single quote completely, so the vendor comparison rests on comparable numbers.

Before you accept a quote

Before accepting, read the quote once more from the top, slowly, as if you had never seen it. Check that the products, units, and packaging match the brief you sent. Check that every worksheet row is accounted for — stated, bundled with a stated scope, or explicitly billed separately. Check that the development position is clear, the testing list is in writing, the freight basis is stated, and the sample rounds are counted. Check that any figure that surprised you has an explanation you understand, in terms of the row it sits in. If anything is unclear, ask now, while you still have the leverage of an uncommitted buyer. Questions asked after acceptance are requests; questions asked before acceptance are part of the negotiation, and good manufacturers expect them. Resist accepting the lowest total without the worksheet behind it: the lowest total is sometimes the best value, and sometimes it is the quote that left the most out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Skincare line costs
Why do two quotes for the same product look so different?

Quotes differ because they include different buckets. One quote may bundle testing, freight, and labels into its total while another leaves them out, so the totals cannot be compared until every quote is broken into the same worksheet rows on the same basis.

What should I do if a quote will not itemise its costs?

Treat it as a warning sign and ask for an itemised version broken into the worksheet rows. If the manufacturer will not itemise, you cannot tell what you are paying for or compare the quote fairly, so keep the conversation open but do not commit on the basis of a single unexplained total.
