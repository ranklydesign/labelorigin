The three-product worked example, unpacked

The pillar guide illustrates a first launch with three stock products, standard packaging, and a fixed unit count for each product. This guide unpacks that illustration line by line, explains what its total leaves out, and shows how the total moves when one assumption changes.

The illustration, stated plainly

The example in the worked example in the skincare line cost guide is an illustration, and it should be read as one. It imagines a launch of three stock-formula products — a cleanser, a moisturizer, and a serum — at 250 units each, with standard packaging. Stock formulas matter here, because the formulas already exist: there is no development work to price, no trial batches to fund, and no revision rounds to budget for. Standard packaging matters too, because it means containers and closures in ordinary forms, without a custom mould, a distinctive finish, or an elaborate outer presentation. The unit count matters most of all, because every per-unit line in the example is multiplied by it. Change the count, and the illustration changes. Change the path, and it changes more.

Every figure in this guide is one of the pillar's own illustrative figures, labelled illustrative wherever it appears, and each one sits within its published band. No other figures are used. That restraint is deliberate: the purpose of unpacking an illustration is to teach how a launch total is built and how it moves, not to supply a second set of numbers a founder might mistake for a price. The illustrative figures are estimates based on general industry patterns — not a quote — and they are presented only so the reasoning can be followed line by line. A founder who needs a price must replace every illustrative line with a real quote, on the same basis, for the same products, units, and packaging. Until that replacement happens, the illustration is a teaching tool, not a budget. The count is the pillar's illustration only, not a manufacturer minimum or threshold.

Line by line

The table below sets out the illustration exactly as the pillar presents it, with each figure labelled illustrative. Read it slowly, line by line. Notice which lines are priced per unit and which are priced as single amounts, which grow when the unit count grows, and which stay fixed. That distinction — between lines that move with units and lines that do not — is the key to understanding sensitivity, and it makes the two sensitivity sections later in this guide intelligible. A total is only as useful as the reader's understanding of the lines beneath it.

Why each line sits where it does

Fill, packaging, and labels

Fill, packaging, and labels are the per-unit lines, and together they form the bulk of the illustrative total. Fill is shown at $4.00 per unit, illustrative, giving $3,000, illustrative, across the three products at 250 units each. Packaging is shown at $1.50 per unit, illustrative, giving $1,125, illustrative, on the same unit count. Labels are shown at $0.40 per label, illustrative, giving $300, illustrative. Each illustrative figure sits within its published band and is a mid-range figure — neither the lowest the band allows nor the highest. Mid-range figures suit an illustration, because they avoid suggesting a launch will be unusually cheap or unusually dear. They show the middle of the picture and leave the founder to discover, through real quotes, where their own products sit within each band.

Testing, samples, freight, and brand setup

Testing is shown as included, at $0, illustrative, because the illustration assumes testing is included in the stock-formula arrangement it imagines. That assumption is part of the illustration, not a general rule. In real quotes, testing is frequently billed separately, and a founder must ask which tests are included and what is billed separately rather than assuming inclusion. The $0 line, illustrative, is therefore a prompt as much as a figure: check, for your own quote, whether testing is included, and if not, what it adds. Samples are shown as one sample round at $150, illustrative — a modest assumption for a stock launch, where the formula already exists. For custom work the assumption differs, and more rounds are usually needed, because the first round almost never ships unchanged.

The total and what it leaves out

The illustrative total of about $5,675, illustrative, is stated before marketing, and that qualifier carries real weight. Launch marketing needs its own separate budget, kept out of the production total, so the founder can see clearly whether the launch can be afforded to make and to sell. An illustration folding marketing into the production total would hide the affordability question. The pillar's total does not do that: it shows the production and setup picture and leaves marketing as a separate decision. A founder using this illustration should do the same — take the illustrative total as a picture of production and setup, and plan marketing separately, without letting the two totals blur.

Fewer units: the direction of movement

The pillar changes one assumption to show sensitivity: the unit count drops to 100 units each. This guide describes that change in direction only, with no new computed totals. The per-unit lines — fill, packaging, and labels — fall in total, because there are fewer units to fill, pack, and label. The fixed lines — freight and brand setup — stay fixed, because they are not priced per unit and do not shrink with the count. The total therefore falls, but only modestly, because the fixed lines hold it up. At the same time, the cost of each unit climbs, because the fixed lines are spread across fewer units and per-unit figures themselves generally sit higher on smaller runs. The founder ends up with a lower total and a higher cost per unit: less cash committed overall, more cash tied up in each unit that must be sold.

That trade-off is the real lesson, and it is why direction matters more than a new total. A founder seeing only that the total falls may conclude a smaller run is simply cheaper, missing the higher cost per unit. A founder seeing only that the cost per unit climbs may conclude it is simply dearer, missing the lower total cash commitment protecting the launch. Both halves must be weighed together: the smaller run protects cash and limits unsold stock, while the larger run lowers the cost of each unit but commits more cash and creates more stock to sell through. Neither is right in the abstract. The right choice depends on realistic sell-through, the founder's cash position, and the manufacturer's minimums — matters for real quotes and real planning, not for an illustration. The move to 100 units each is not a recommendation; it is a demonstration that the total is sensitive to the count, and that sensitivity runs in both directions at once.

Switching to custom: the direction of movement

The pillar's second sensitivity changes the path: the same launch, switched from stock formulas to custom formulation. Again, this guide describes the movement in direction only, with no new computed totals. The direction is upward, and it moves before production starts. Custom formulation adds development work — chemist time, trial batches, and revision rounds — that the stock path does not carry, billed before a single unit is filled, so the total rises by the development work on top of the production lines that remain. The founder pays for creating formulas as well as making products, and the illustration's stock-only total cannot be carried across unchanged.

What to take from the example

Take the structure, and leave the figures. The structure is per-unit lines multiplied by units per product, fixed lines that do not move with units, a total that is the sum of both, and a clear statement of what the total leaves out. That structure survives every change of assumption, because it is how launch totals are built. Replace the illustrative figures with real quotes, line by line, on the same basis, add the lines the illustration omits if your launch includes them, and the total that emerges will be yours, grounded in real figures. The launch cost calculator computes ranges from inputs you supply, using the same published bands the pillar uses; use it to explore how different inputs move the picture, and use real quotes to settle the figures for your own launch.

Take the sensitivity lesson, and hold it as direction. Units and path drive the budget, not the product idea: fewer units move the total down modestly and the cost of each unit up, while a custom path moves the total up and moves spending earlier. Those directions are the durable truths of the example and will hold for your launch even though the illustrative figures will not. A launch total is not a single number to memorise; it is a sum of lines, each with its own basis, scope, and sensitivity. The founder who understands the lines can change a choice and foresee the direction of the change. Read the illustration that way, replace its figures with real quotes, and let your own total — built line by line, on evidence — be the number you plan around. That total will not be about $5,675, illustrative. It will be yours, and it will be real.

What changes the total most if I change one assumption?

Units and path move the total most. The direction of movement is the lesson; the size of the movement depends on real quotes, not on this illustration.
