Selling skincare in your spa: what the rules actually say in all 50 states, DC and Puerto Rico

49 of 52 jurisdictions require a shop license to sell skincare in a salon or med spa — and none explicitly require a separate retail license.

We read the official rules in all 50 states, DC and Puerto Rico (52 jurisdictions) and counted what they actually say. The full table is on our state-rules page — these are the six findings that come out of it.

Two things before the findings. First, on the retail license, the honest version is narrower than the hook: no board’s site states that one is required — but only 3 of 52 say no outright, and the other 49 do not mention one. Second, these are national counts. To check your state, go straight to the full table.

Two filings — every jurisdiction needs at least one

Every jurisdiction in the table requires at least one of the two filings — and 42 of 52 require both. The shop or facility license is the permission to operate the room; the sales-tax permit is the permission to collect tax on the sale. They come from different agencies, and neither agency’s site mentions the other’s requirement.

42 both · 7 shop-only · 3 tax-only · 0 neither.

Three jurisdictions have no salon license at all

Connecticut, Utah, and Puerto Rico have no state-level salon or shop license — but they are three different situations, not one rule. And none of the three is license-free: each still licenses practitioners, and each still requires a sales-tax registration.

JurisdictionWhat the row saysWhat replaces the license
Connecticut“No state-level shop license — premises licensed by the local health district”Practitioners licensed by the state; premises licensed by the local health district. Sales and Use Tax Permit required.
Utah“No state-level salon/shop license; the Cosmetology Act licenses practitioners and schools only”Practitioners and schools licensed; no shop license at any level the row states. Sales tax license required.
Puerto Rico“No board-issued salon/shop license; the board licenses individual practitioners only (Ley 431; Reglamento 8663)”Individual practitioners licensed. Certificado de Registro de Comerciantes (IVU) via SURI required.

Sales tax in 45 of 52 — and the 7 without it are not tax-free

45 of 52 jurisdictions require a sales-tax permit for retail skincare sales. The 7 that do not split two ways — and neither way means the seller pays nothing.

45 sales tax · 3 other levy · 4 none.

Count the gross receipts and excise filings too, and 47 of 52 jurisdictions require a state tax registration of some kind. Delaware is the one exception: its tax applies but no registration step is named, so it is not counted. The 4 not counted are Alaska, Oregon, Montana, and New Hampshire.

No board’s site says a separate retail license is required

In no jurisdiction did the board’s site state that a separate retail license is required. That is the finding — and the honest version of it is narrower than it sounds: only three boards say no outright. The other 49 say nothing at all, and silence is not proof.

California is the only row where the state’s official business guide enumerates the full requirement list: it “lists only the establishment license and a seller’s permit.” Utah and Puerto Rico say no the same way — the board “licenses practitioners, not salons, so there is no retail add-on.” So the claim is not “no jurisdiction requires a separate retail license.” It is “in no jurisdiction did the board’s site state that one is required” — and your state’s row is the place to check.

The names change at every border

The same two filings go by different names in different states. The shop license is a salon license in one state, an establishment license in another, a shop registration in a third. The sales-tax permit is a seller’s permit, a Certificate of Authority, a vendor’s license, or an FR-500 — depending on the state. That is why the table names the agency and the permit for every row, instead of asking you to guess what your state calls it.

  • Salon license
  • Establishment license
  • Shop registration
  • Seller’s permit
  • Certificate of Authority
  • Vendor’s license
  • FR-500

One more split worth knowing: in 4 of 52 jurisdictions the shop is licensed through a health department, not a cosmetology board or a licensing department — Arkansas, Connecticut, Nebraska, and Rhode Island. Oregon’s Health Licensing Office does not count: it is a licensing office, not a health department.

One state draws the salon/med-spa line in writing. Illinois is the only state in the table whose regulator has published the split: a joint memo from the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation (IDFPR) and the Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH), updated 30 October 2025, states that a salon registration issued by IDFPR cannot be used to operate a med spa, and that medical procedures fall within physician scope. Read the memo (IDFPR, PDF, updated 30 October 2025). The memo is about med spa services, not retail skincare — it does not change the Illinois row above (salon Certificate of Registration, sales-tax Certificate of Registration, no separate retail license stated). But for a spa that does both, it is the clearest statement in the country that the retail side and the medical side need separate permissions from separate regulators.

Method and sources

Every row of the table was checked against the official state board and revenue page — the licensing body and the license it issues from the board’s own site or the official state code, and the sales-tax permit and the agency that issues it from the revenue department’s own site. Rows were last verified 31 October, 1 November, or 2 November 2026, and the table is rechecked every month. Where the board’s site does not mention a separate retail license, the cell says so rather than inferring a rule — the one exception is California, where the state’s official business guide enumerates the full requirement list, so the cell says no. Frequently Asked Questions

The 52-jurisdiction rules
Do I need a separate retail license to sell skincare in my spa?

In no verified jurisdiction did the board's site state that a separate retail license is required. Three jurisdictions say no outright — California, Utah, and Puerto Rico — and the other 49 boards do not mention one. Silence is not proof, so check your state's row in the full table before you act.

Which states have no salon license?

Three jurisdictions have no state-level salon or shop license: Connecticut, Utah, and Puerto Rico — and they are three different situations. Connecticut licenses premises through the local health district; Utah licenses practitioners and schools only; Puerto Rico licenses individual practitioners only. All three still require a sales-tax registration.

Do I need a sales-tax permit to sell skincare?

In 45 of 52 jurisdictions, yes. The 7 without a sales tax are not tax-free: 4 have no state-level levy on the sale — Alaska, Oregon, Montana, and New Hampshire — and 3 replace it with a gross receipts or excise tax — Delaware, New Mexico, and Hawaii. Count those filings too, and 47 of 52 require a state tax registration of some kind.

How was this data checked?

Every row of the table was checked against the official state board and revenue page, and last verified 31 October, 1 November, or 2 November 2026. The table is rechecked every month, and each row links to its official source so you can verify the current wording yourself.

Can I use a salon registration to operate a med spa in Illinois?

No. A joint memo from Illinois's IDFPR and Department of Public Health, updated 30 October 2025, states that a salon registration issued by IDFPR cannot be used to operate a med spa. Medical procedures fall within physician scope. The memo is about med spa services, not retail skincare — Illinois still requires a salon Certificate of Registration and a sales-tax Certificate of Registration to sell skincare.
