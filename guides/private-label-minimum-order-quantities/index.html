Private label skincare minimum order quantities, explained

Minimum order quantity is the first filter a buyer applies — and the most common reason a private-label inquiry dies quietly. A minimum that is too high kills a launch before it starts; a minimum you cannot sell through turns into inventory that expires on the shelf. This guide explains what MOQs are, why they are counted per SKU, what the published market ranges actually are, and how to size a first order you can sell through.

What are typical minimum order quantities for private-label skincare?

Published minimums for private-label skincare run from 6 units per SKU to 5,000 units per SKU, with most US low-MOQ programs between 12 and 50 units per SKU. The table below lists 18 published MOQs with sources and dates. Always confirm the figure in writing: the minimum is per SKU, and packaging can carry its own minimum.

A minimum order quantity is the smallest number of units a manufacturer will produce of one product in one production run. It is the single biggest filter when you are starting out, because it determines your cash outlay and your inventory risk: the minimum decides how much money you commit before you have sold a single unit, and how many units you have to sell before the launch pays for itself.

Minimums exist because production runs have fixed costs that do not shrink with the order. Setting up a fill line, sourcing components, and printing labels cost roughly the same whether the run is 25 units or 2,500 — so the per-unit cost of a tiny run is far higher than the per-unit cost of a large one. A manufacturer that goes below its batch size loses money on the run. That is why the minimum is a property of the production process, not a negotiating tactic, and why it varies so much between manufacturers: their lines, their component suppliers, and their batch sizes are different.

Per SKU, not per order

The minimum applies to each product separately, not to the order as a whole. A SKU is one formula in one size in one fragrance — and minimums can apply separately to each formula, fragrance, and size, rather than to the collection as a whole. Ask how the manufacturer defines a SKU before you count anything.

The arithmetic catches people out. Trade guidance for spas gives the example plainly: three scents with a minimum of 250 units each create a 750-unit commitment, not a 250-unit one. A three-product launch at 500 units per SKU is a 1,500-unit order. Ask whether any minimums can be combined across products, then compare the real commitment with your storage space, your budget, and your realistic sales expectations — not with the number on the first page of the quote.

Count the commitment, not the minimum. Multiply the per-SKU minimum by every SKU on your shortlist — every formula, fragrance, and size — before you compare it with your budget.

The published market ranges

There is no single industry MOQ. What you will be quoted depends on where the manufacturer sits: overseas volume factories, mid-size OEM/ODM plants, or US low-MOQ programs built for small brands. The published ranges below come from the manufacturers and programs named — they are market ranges, not a quote from any one manufacturer, and Renaud Naturals’ own minimums are confirmed in writing on request, not published here.

Overseas factories: 500 to 3,000 units per SKU

Volume factories in cosmetics manufacturing clusters typically quote private label on existing formulas from 500 units per SKU, with standard OEM production around 1,000 units and custom formulation from 3,000 units per SKU and up — the figures published by Skincraftlab, a Zhongshan, Guangdong OEM/ODM manufacturer, and echoed across the market: GZ Cosmetics Lab puts the typical private-label range at 1,000 to 3,000 units per SKU, and Korean OEM/ODM guides put private label at 300 to 1,000 units per SKU for ready formulas, with custom development from 1,000. These are real minimums for real production lines — and they are why an overseas quote that looks cheap per unit can still be the wrong first order for a spa.

US low-MOQ programs: 12 to 25 units per SKU

Programs built for small US brands publish much lower thresholds. SlimSpa, a spa-focused private-label supplier, lists low minimum orders of 12 units per item. Beauty Branding Lab, a US low-MOQ private-label program, publishes 25 units per SKU for stock formulas with eligible in-stock packaging, with a $2,500 development fee and a 250-unit minimum for custom formulation over a typical 12-week timeline. Aesthetic Back Bar, another US program aimed at estheticians and small spas, advertises no minimum order and a $99 starter set, with its published guide putting the floor at 6 units per SKU and a 2-to-3-week turnaround. The pattern is consistent: US programs built for small buyers start in the tens of units per SKU, not the hundreds.

The gap between those two worlds is the whole decision. A 500-unit minimum at a low per-unit price is still 500 units to store, sell, and move before they expire. A 25-unit minimum at a higher per-unit price is a test you can afford to run. For a first line, the minimum you can sell through beats the minimum that looks cheapest per unit.

Why minimums exist

Three costs set the floor. First, the fill line: setting up, cleaning, and calibrating a production run costs the same whether it fills 25 bottles or 2,500. Second, components: packaging suppliers have their own minimums, so a manufacturer ordering bottles, pumps, and caps for your run is itself buying in bulk. Third, labels: print runs have minimum quantities, and a custom label for 25 units costs far more per label than the same label printed for 1,000.

That is also why lower minimums mean higher per-unit cost: the fixed costs are spread across fewer units. It is not a penalty. It is arithmetic. And it is why comparing two quotes by per-unit price alone misleads: the quote with the lower per-unit price usually carries the higher minimum, and the total commitment is what your budget has to cover.

Packaging changes the minimum

The same formula can carry a different minimum depending on how it is packed. Beauty Branding Lab publishes its tiers plainly: 25 or more units per SKU with eligible stock packaging, 100 or more for airless and premium packaging, and generally 500 or more where expanded custom component sourcing is involved. Custom containers, metallic finishes, airless pumps, and printed components each carry their own minimums and their own lead times.

Stock packaging in standard sizes is the cheapest way to start small: the components already exist, the minimums are the lowest, and the turnaround is the fastest. Custom packaging is a second launch, not a first-launch decision. Pick the product first, the stock packaging second, and revisit custom components once the line is selling and the first reorders are in.

How to size your first order

Start from sell-through, not from the minimum. Estimate realistic monthly sales per SKU — how many units your team will actually recommend and sell in a normal month — and multiply by three to four months of cover. Add testers and staff use: every product on the shelf needs testers in the treatment room, and your team needs to use the products to recommend them honestly. Then check the total against the per-SKU minimum: if the minimum is far above what you can sell in four months, the SKU is too big for a first order, however good the formula is. If every minimum you are seeing is above what you can sell through, our guide to low minimum order quantities explains what counts as low, and when a low minimum is worth the higher per-unit price.

Watch expiry. Unsold inventory is not just tied-up cash; skincare expires, and a first order that is still on the shelf a year later is a loss, not an asset. Our guide to unsold inventory and expiry risk covers the math. Reorder minimums usually match first-order minimums, so plan the second run before the first sells out — a line that sells through and reorders is a line, and a gap on the shelf is a client buying something else.

Questions to ask before you commit

Ask these in writing, before you commit to anything:

  • Is the minimum per SKU or per order — and how do you define a SKU?
  • Can minimums be combined across products, fragrances, or sizes?
  • What are the packaging tiers, and how does each one change the minimum?
  • Do reorder minimums match first-order minimums?
  • What testing is included in the quote, and what is billed separately?
  • What happens if a formula is discontinued — do I get notice, and can I buy a final run?
