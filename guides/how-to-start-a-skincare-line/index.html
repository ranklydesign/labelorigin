How to Start a Skincare Line

Disclosure: Renaud Naturals is LabelOrigin's flagship client — Renaud pays LabelOrigin to bring it wholesale buyers. LabelOrigin doesn't sell product; Renaud quotes and fulfills.

Starting a skincare line means choosing between private label (an existing formula under your brand) and custom formulation, then working out your margins, checking your state's licensing and sales-tax rules, sampling the product, and getting labels and claims right before you launch. Most of the cost and delay sits in the minimums and the sampling round, not the branding.

Private label vs custom formulation — the fork

Everything downstream depends on this choice, so make it first.

Private label means you take a manufacturer's existing formula and put your label on it. You pick from their catalogue, you adjust the scent, the packaging, maybe the active at a low level — and the formula itself is proven. It is the faster, cheaper way in, and it is what most med spas, salons, and first-time founders actually do.

Custom formulation means a formula built for you from scratch. You own something nobody else sells, and you pay for it in development time, testing, and a higher first order. It fits a founder with a specific product idea that no catalogue formula matches — a particular active, a texture, a claim.

Neither is the "real" way to do it. The honest test is: does a catalogue formula already do what you want to sell? If yes, private label gets you to market months sooner. If no, custom is the only honest route — and you should budget for the development round, not just the first production run.

Money first — margins and budget

Do this before you fall in love with a product. A skincare line lives or dies on margin, and the margin is decided by four numbers: what the unit costs you, what you sell it for, what the minimum order forces you to hold, and what it costs to get it to the shelf.

Work it out on the calculator before you talk to any manufacturer: private-label margin calculator. Put in your real unit cost and your real retail price, not the ones you hope for. If the margin doesn't survive a realistic retail price, no amount of good branding saves it.

The budget has five buckets: samples, first production run, labels and packaging, compliance (labels, claims, and any testing your products need), and launch marketing. Founders consistently under-budget the first two — the sample round and the minimum order — because those are the ones that feel optional until they aren't. They aren't. A sample round you skip is a product you launch blind.

One rule that holds across every manufacturer we track: never commit to a first order before you have costed the second one. Reorder minimums and lead times decide whether the line survives past launch, and they are rarely the same as the first-order terms. Our MOQ explainer covers how minimums actually work: low MOQ skincare, explained.

For the budget itself, the skincare line cost guide breaks launch costs into buckets with published ranges, and the launch cost calculator turns them into a first-run estimate for your own products and units.

Brand and products — pick a narrow start

A skincare line is not a catalogue. The lines that work start narrow: two to five products that solve one problem for one customer. A cleanser, a serum, and a moisturiser for post-treatment skin. A body-care set for a spa's retail shelf. One hero product and the two things people buy with it.

Pick your customer before your products. A med spa's retail customer is not a boutique hotel's amenity buyer, and neither is an esthetician's regular. The customer decides the price point, the packaging, and the claims you can honestly make — so if you can't describe the customer in one sentence, you aren't ready to pick products.

Then match the products to the customer, not the other way round. Ask: what does this person already buy, what would they switch for, and what would they pay? If you can't answer all three, keep narrowing until you can.

State rules — licensing and sales tax, before you sell a unit

This is the step most start-a-line guides skip, and it's the one that actually stops people. Whether you need a shop or salon license, and whether you owe sales tax on retail sales, changes state by state — and almost nobody publishes it in one place.

Labels and claims — Drug Facts, MoCRA, and what you can say

Skincare labels are regulated, and the line between a cosmetic claim and a drug claim is a legal one, not a marketing one. If your product says it treats, heals, or changes the structure of skin, it is making a drug claim — and drug claims bring a different set of rules, including Drug Facts labelling.

Before you approve any label, know three things:

  • What your product is, legally. A cosmetic cleanses or beautifies. A drug claims to treat or prevent. The words on your label decide which one you are.
  • What MoCRA requires of you as the brand owner. The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act puts duties on the brand owner — facility registration, product listing, adverse-event reporting, and safety substantiation among them. Our guide for brand owners lays out the duties: MoCRA brand owner duties. If you sell through a spa, the spa-side rules are here: MoCRA rules for spas selling skincare.
  • What you can honestly claim. A claim you can't substantiate is a liability, not a selling point. If the formula doesn't do it, the label shouldn't say it — no matter how good it sounds.

Get the label right before production, not after. Reprinting labels is the most avoidable cost in this whole process.

Sample — the round you don't skip

Order samples before you commit to anything. A sample is the only way to know what you're actually selling: the texture, the scent, the way it sits on skin, the way the packaging feels in a hand.

When you sample, test like a customer, not like a founder. Use it for a week. Give it to two people who fit your target customer and ask them what they'd pay for it — not whether they like it. "I like it" costs you nothing; "I'd pay this much" tells you whether the line works.

Ask the manufacturer for the current sample terms and sample pricing in writing before you order — what's included, what it costs, and how long it takes.

If a manufacturer won't let you sample before a first order, that is information. The manufacturers worth working with want you to try the product first — a sample round protects them too, because it catches the mismatch before production.

Packaging and identity — the label is the product, to the buyer

Your customer meets the packaging before the formula. The bottle, the box, the label — that is the product as far as the shelf is concerned. So packaging deserves the same care as the formula, and it deserves it early, because packaging lead times are routinely longer than production lead times.

Three practical points:

  • Design for the shelf you'll actually be on. A med spa shelf, a salon counter, and an online store are three different contexts. The label has to read in all of them.
  • Keep the claim and the design honest. The label design should say what the product does, not what you wish it did. (See section 5.)
  • Budget for the design itself — ask for current design pricing before you commit.

Minimums — what the real numbers are

Minimum order quantities decide how much cash you need on day one, and they vary wildly between manufacturers — which is why you should never take a single MOQ figure as "the" number for the industry.

Published figures from manufacturers' own sites (checked Nov 2, 2026; the full table is in our listicle, rechecked Oct 31, 2026):

  • Onoxa publishes a minimum of 12 units per SKU on its own site. Its sample kits are published at $58.95–$139.95, and its full-service design at $199.99 (Onoxa's own site, checked Nov 2, 2026).
  • Trilogy Laboratories publishes "from 25 units" on its med-spa page (checked Nov 2, 2026). It does not publish production prices — only $3.99 per-product samples.

Those are other manufacturers' published numbers, and they are the honest benchmark: if a manufacturer quotes you far above them for a stock formula, ask what the extra buys you. If it quotes far below, ask what's missing. Approve before production — the checkpoint that saves the line

Before a single unit is produced, approve four things in writing: the final formula (after sampling), the final label (after the claims check), the packaging (after seeing a physical sample, not a render), and the order terms (minimum, price, lead time, reorder terms).

This is the checkpoint most first-time founders rush, and it's the one that costs the most when it's skipped. A formula you haven't sampled, a label you haven't read against the regulations, packaging you've only seen on screen — each one is a rework waiting to happen, and rework after production is the most expensive kind.

If anything in the four approvals isn't right, stop the line. A week's delay now is cheaper than a production run you can't sell.

A real timeline — how long it actually takes

Nobody publishes a reliable end-to-end timeline, because the timeline depends on the manufacturer, the formula, and how many sample rounds you need. What we can say honestly is where the time goes: sampling, label and packaging approval, production, and the compliance checks in between. Sampling and approvals are the long poles — production itself is usually the shortest part.

Ask for the current turnaround, formulation timing and cost, and the timeline from first order to retail — in writing — at the same time you ask for the minimum, and then add a buffer for your own approvals. The founders who launch on time are the ones who budgeted for the approval rounds, not the ones who assumed a single pass.

Launch — the first 30 days

Launch narrow, and launch where you already have trust. If you're a med spa, your first customers are the clients already in your chair. If you're an esthetician, they're the regulars who already ask what you use. Sell to the people who trust you before you spend on reaching strangers.

The first 30 days have one job: prove the product sells twice. A first sale proves curiosity; a second sale — the same customer coming back, or a new customer from a referral — proves the product works. Until you have that, don't expand the range. Don't add SKUs to fix a product that isn't selling; fix the product, the price, or the customer you're selling it to.

Frequently Asked Questions

Starting a skincare line
How much does it cost to start a skincare line?

It depends on the minimum order, the samples, the labels and packaging, and the launch — and the minimum is usually the biggest single cost. Work out your real numbers on our margin calculator before you commit to anything.

What is the minimum order for private label skincare?

It varies by manufacturer. Onoxa publishes 12 units per SKU and Trilogy publishes from 25 units (both on their own sites, checked Nov 2, 2026). For Renaud Naturals, ask for the current minimums in writing — they are confirmed per order, not published as a fixed figure.

Private label or custom formulation — which should I choose?

Private label if a catalogue formula already does what you want to sell — it's faster and cheaper. Custom if you have a specific product no catalogue formula matches, and you're ready to pay for development time and a higher first order. Section 1 above walks through the choice.

Do I need a license to sell skincare in my med spa?

It depends on your state. We verified the licensing and sales-tax rules for all 50 states, DC and Puerto Rico (52 jurisdictions) against each state's official source: state rules for selling skincare in your med spa.

How long does it take to start a skincare line?

Sampling and approvals take the longest; production itself is usually the shortest part. Ask your manufacturer for the current timeline in writing — and add a buffer for your own approval rounds.
