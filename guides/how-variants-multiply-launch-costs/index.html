How variants multiply every cost in a launch

A new scent, size, or shade sounds like a small addition to a product that already exists. Follow it through every cost bucket, and it behaves like a new product in miniature.

Updated 28 October 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges below as starting points for your own quotes.

A multiplier, not an add-on

Founders tend to think of variants as add-ons: a product exists, and a variant is a modest variation on it, priced as a modest extra. The cost guide tells a different story. A variant — a different scent, a different size, a different shade — generally needs its own handling in every bucket that the original product carries. That is why this guide describes variants as multipliers rather than add-ons: each variant multiplies the lines that a single product carries once, across fill, packaging, labels, and testing exposure. The point is developed in how variants multiply costs in the skincare line cost guide, where variant lines appear in the per-unit buckets and in the worked example's per-product framing.

The multiplier idea is easy to grasp once the per-product framing is clear. Minimums are usually set per product, and a variant is treated, for production purposes, as a distinct product to be run, packed, and labelled. Its units are counted separately, its label run is produced separately, and its testing exposure is considered separately. The variant may share a brand, a container family, and a place in the lineup with the original product, but it does not share the original product's run. It has a run of its own, and a run of its own carries the lines of a run. A founder who budgets a variant as a small extra on the original product's lines will therefore under-budget it in every bucket at once.

One variant through every bucket

The clearest way to see the multiplier is to trace a single variant through every affected bucket. Take a moisturiser offered in a second scent. The scent change looks small from the buyer's side — the same cream, a different fragrance — but from the production side it touches fill, packaging, labels, and testing, each in its own way. The published bands for those lines are: fill at $1.50–$12.00 per unit (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote), packaging at $0.50–$5.00 per unit (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote), labels at $0.10–$1.00 per label (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote), and testing at $500–$3,000 per formula (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote). Those bands are stated here once, as the lines the variant multiplies; the rest of this guide refers to the lines by name.

Fill and packaging

The variant needs its own fill run, because the product in the container is different: a different scent means a different formula to be filled, even where the base is shared. The fill line therefore applies to the variant's units at the variant's unit count, not as a share of the original product's fill line. Packaging follows the same logic. The variant's units need containers and closures of their own, and where the variant uses a different size, the container itself differs as well. Even where the container is identical, the variant's units still consume containers unit by unit, so the packaging line multiplies with the variant's run. A variant that shares packaging with the original product still multiplies the packaging line; it simply multiplies it at the same per-unit position within the band.

Labels

Labels are where the multiplier is most visible, because a variant almost always needs its own label run. The label must name the variant correctly — its scent, size, or shade, and its ingredient listing as it applies to that variant — and a label run for the original product cannot be reused for a product that differs in those respects. Each variant therefore carries its own label line, at its own unit count, with its own setup and handling. The label band applies per label, so the label line for the variant is the band position for that variant multiplied by the variant's units. Founders who count labels once for a product and its variants together will miss the label line for every variant after the first.

Testing exposure

Testing exposure is the bucket founders most often overlook for variants, because testing feels like a property of the original product rather than of each version of it. The cost guide's framing is more careful: a variant carries its own testing exposure, because the testing that applies to a formula must be considered for the variant as it will actually be sold. Whether testing is included in a quoted price or billed separately varies by manufacturer, and the founder must confirm the treatment for each variant rather than assuming the original product's testing covers every version of it. Ask for the test list in writing, variant by variant, so the exposure is visible before the variant is committed to.

Minimums

Minimums complete the trace, because they set the floor beneath the variant's run. A minimum is usually set per product, and a variant treated as a distinct product carries a minimum of its own. The founder must therefore order at least the minimum for the variant, even if the expected demand for that variant is modest, and must fund the fill, packaging, and label lines for that minimum run. That floor is what makes a small variant expensive in relative terms: the variant's lines cannot be scaled down below its minimum, however small the variant's expected audience. A variant with a modest following can still be worthwhile, but only when the founder has seen the minimum and judged the run against realistic movement for that variant specifically.

Trace the variant, bucket by bucket. Before adding any variant, walk it through fill, packaging, labels, testing, and minimums, and confirm each line in writing for that variant specifically.

Scent, size, and shade

Not all variants multiply in exactly the same way, and the differences are worth naming, because they affect which lines move most. A scent variant changes the formula as sold, so its fill and testing exposure move most clearly, alongside its own label run. A size variant changes the container and the amount of product in each unit, so its packaging and fill handling move most clearly, alongside a label that must state the new size correctly. A shade variant changes the product as presented and as labelled, so its label and fill handling move most clearly, alongside the testing consideration for the variant as sold. The pattern is the same in each case — every bucket is touched — but the emphasis differs, and the founder should ask which lines a given variant moves most.

The less visible variant costs

Beyond the named buckets, variants carry less visible costs. Each variant must be tracked separately, presented separately so buyers can distinguish it, and learned from separately, with thinner selling evidence for each version than a single product would give. Those costs grow with the variant count, even though no single line on a quote names them, and a launch with many variants can make it harder to tell which version is moving and why.

Keeping the variant count deliberate

A deliberate variant count starts from the smallest lineup that can represent the brand honestly, and adds variants only where there is a reason to believe buyers want them. The reason should be specific: buyers asking for a scent, a size that a route requires, a shade that completes a range a stockist expects. A variant added because it is easy to imagine, or because a competitor carries it, multiplies every line without a comparable reason to believe it will move. The founder's task is to distinguish the variants buyers have asked for from the variants the founder can merely picture, and to fund the first group before the second.

Deliberation also means sequencing. Variants need not all launch together. A founder can launch with a focused lineup, learn which products move and what buyers ask for, and add variants in a later run, when the evidence for each variant is stronger and the funds for its multiplication are clearer. Sequencing does not avoid the multiplier — a later variant still carries its own lines — but it places the multiplication after learning, rather than before it. That order is kinder to the budget and kinder to the founder's attention, because each variant added later is added with a reason that the first launch has supplied. Questions before adding a variant

Before adding any variant, a founder should be able to answer a short list of questions that make the multiplication visible. Each question corresponds to a bucket the variant will touch, and each answer should be confirmed with the manufacturer in writing for that variant specifically, rather than assumed from the original product. A variant that survives those questions is a variant worth funding; a variant that cannot answer them is a variant to postpone.

  • What is the minimum for this variant, and how many units will it require? Confirm the variant's own minimum and size its run to realistic movement for that variant, not for the product family as a whole.
  • What fill and packaging lines will the variant carry? Confirm the per-unit lines for the variant's units, and whether a size change alters the container or the fill handling.
  • What label run will the variant need? Confirm that the variant needs its own label run, with its scent, size, or shade and its ingredient listing stated correctly for that variant.
  • What testing exposure does the variant carry? Ask for the test list in writing for the variant as sold, and confirm whether testing is included or billed separately.
  • What evidence supports this variant? Name the buyer request, route requirement, or selling evidence that justifies multiplying every line for this version rather than another.

A variant is not an enemy of a good launch. A well-chosen variant can complete a lineup, serve a buyer who would otherwise go elsewhere, and give a launch the breadth it needs. But a variant is a multiplier, and multipliers deserve respect. Trace each one through fill, packaging, labels, testing, and minimums; fund each one on its own lines; and add each one only when the evidence for it is stronger than the ease of imagining it. A launch built that way will have fewer variants, and each variant it has will have earned its place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Variants and launch costs
Is a variant just a small extra on an existing product?

No. A variant usually needs its own run, its own label run, and its own testing exposure, so it multiplies the lines the original product carries rather than adding a small extra to them.

Should a first launch include many variants?

Usually it should not. Starting with fewer variants keeps each cost line simpler, and further variants can be added later once the first products have shown what buyers want.
