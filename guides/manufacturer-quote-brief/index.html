What to send a manufacturer to get an accurate quote

Manufacturers quote from specifics, not from ideas. This guide explains each item in a complete brief — product, units, packaging, target price, and timeline — and how each one shapes the price you are quoted.

Updated 28 October 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges below as starting points for your own quotes.

Why an idea alone is not enough

A founder often begins with a sentence: I want a moisturiser, or a cleanser that feels luxurious. The sentence feels clear to the founder, who can picture the product, the jar, and the buyer holding it. To a manufacturer, the same sentence is almost empty. It does not say what kind of moisturiser, how much of it, in what container, for what selling price, or by when. Each missing detail changes the work, the materials, and the cost, and a manufacturer asked to quote from a sentence alone must either guess, or quote so broadly that the figure means very little. Neither outcome helps a founder make a decision.

The brief turns an idea into something quotable. It answers, in plain terms, what product, in what format, in what quantity, in what pack, at what target price, and on what timescale. None of those answers needs to be final; a founder can state a preference and invite the manufacturer to suggest an alternative. What matters is that the manufacturer is quoting a defined piece of work, not an aspiration. This guide expands getting an accurate quote in the skincare line cost guide, which sets out the brief list in short form. The brief itself produces figures, so this guide deliberately contains none: any figure stated here would mislead, because every manufacturer prices differently and every product carries its own costs. Send the brief, and let the quotes supply the figures.

Product type and format

Product type and format are the foundation of the brief, because they determine what the manufacturer is actually being asked to make. Type means the kind of product: a cleanser, a moisturiser, a serum, a balm, or a toner. Format means the physical form that type takes: a cream, a gel, an oil, a lotion, or a foam. Products that sound similar can involve quite different making processes and materials. A cleanser that foams and one that does not are different pieces of work, and a serum in a dropper bottle and a serum in a pump bottle are different pieces of work. Naming type and format lets the manufacturer picture the bench work rather than guessing at it.

Units per product

Units per product is the number that drives the quote more than any other single item in the brief. It means the count of finished units for each product in the launch, counted per product rather than across the brand as a whole. Each count is priced separately, because minimums, fill work, packaging, and labels are all set per product. A founder who states only a total across the launch leaves the manufacturer to divide that total, and the division chosen may not match the division intended. State the count for each product plainly, and the quote that comes back will be built on the same counts.

The count should be sized to realistic sell-through: how many units of each product you can sell, through the channels you actually have, in the time the stock will stay fresh. It is tempting to state a larger count, because larger counts can bring the cost of each unit down. But stock that does not sell is not a bargain; it is cash on a shelf with an expiry date, and the saving on each unit disappears if the extra units never sell. Variants deserve separate counts as well. A scent, size, or shade variant is a separate product for quoting purposes, with its own minimum, label run, and pile of stock. Give each variant its own line, even if the count is modest, so the founder can see, variant by variant, what the launch really costs.

Packaging preferences and stock options

Packaging preferences tell the manufacturer what the product should arrive in, and they move the price more than many founders expect. A preference might be a jar, bottle, pump, dropper, or tube, in a stated size, with a stated closure, and it might include an outer carton, an insert, or a seal. Each component carries its own cost and supplier minimum, and each has to be sourced, checked, and filled. A brief naming the preferred pack lets the manufacturer price the real container and closure, rather than an assumed one. A brief leaving packaging vague forces the manufacturer to assume a standard pack, and the quote may describe a presentation the founder would never choose, at a price that cannot be compared with a quote built on a different assumption.

If you have no strong preference, say so and ask for stock options: containers and closures in standard sizes, already available from suppliers, rather than containers made to a custom mould or finish. Stock options are generally the most straightforward route for a first launch, because they avoid the longer lead times and larger commitments custom containers usually require. Asking for them is not a sign of low ambition; it keeps the first launch manageable and leaves the door open to a more distinctive pack later, when sales evidence justifies the commitment. State too whether you want an outer carton, inserts, or seals, and any finish or material preference that matters to you. Unstated preferences surface as surprises, and surprises surface as costs.

Target retail price

Target retail price means the price you intend to charge the end buyer. Founders sometimes hesitate to state it, feeling it reveals their thinking. In practice, withholding it makes the quote less useful. The target price tells the manufacturer what kind of product, pack, and presentation the market must support: a product intended to sell at a modest price needs a different fill, pack, and finish from one intended to sell at a premium price. Without the target, the manufacturer may quote a product that is beautifully made and impossible to sell at the price you intended. State the target for each product, not merely a general sense of where the range sits, because products in a range often carry different targets and a single average hides the differences that matter.

Timeline

Timeline means when you need the finished goods, and what must happen before they can be shipped. It is a sequence — samples to evaluate, revisions to approve, production to schedule, shipment to arrange — and each step depends on the one before it. A brief stating only a launch date leaves the manufacturer to guess how much room the sequence really has. A brief stating the date and inviting the manufacturer to set out a realistic sequence gives them something to respond to honestly: the date may be achievable, it may need to move, or a different path may fit the timescale better. Honesty about timeline also protects the founder from optimism. A date set by a trade show or seasonal moment can feel immovable, tempting the founder to compress the sequence, and compression usually shows up in quality, rework, or a launch that arrives late anyway.

Changes that alter the work

Beyond the core brief items, changes that alter the work itself belong in the brief from the start. A fragrance-free requirement changes the formula work, the raw materials, and the testing picture, and a manufacturer learning of it late must redo work already priced. Outer cartons add a component, a supplier, and an assembly step, and a quote written without them understates the launch. A change of format, size, or an added variant has the same effect. Each reopens part of the quote, and each is far cheaper to include at the brief stage than to add afterwards. List such items separately, as additional requirements, rather than burying them in general description, and label any that are preferences rather than firm needs, so the manufacturer can price the brief with and without them and the founder can see what the preference costs.

Changes arriving after the brief has been sent should be treated as revisions, not asides. Send the revised brief in full, with the change marked, and ask for a revised quote on the same basis. That discipline keeps every quote comparable and the record clean: the founder can see what changed, when, and what it did to the price. A founder who revises by phone call or passing message, without updating the written brief, ends up with quotes describing different pieces of work and no reliable way to tell which is which. The written brief is the anchor. Keep it current, keep it shared, and let every quote hang from the same anchor.

The same brief to every manufacturer

Send the same brief to every manufacturer: the same products, formats, units per product, packaging preferences or stock-option request, target prices, timeline, and additional requirements. Sameness is what makes quotes comparable. Without it, a founder compares answers to different questions, and the totals mislead. With it, differences between quotes become informative, showing different scopes, approaches, or value on a common basis, and the founder can ask why one quote differs from another, with the answer being about the work rather than a briefing gap. Sameness also respects the manufacturers: one receiving a complete, consistent brief can quote promptly and accurately, and show their best work.

Keep a copy of the brief exactly as sent, alongside every quote that comes back. When a quote arrives, check it against the brief item by item before checking its total: does it answer the products as stated, price the units as stated, cover the packaging as stated, reflect the target prices and timeline, and address the additional requirements? A quote answering the brief completely can be compared; one answering it partially needs follow-up pointing to the specific item missed. That habit — brief, quote, check, follow up — is the whole of getting an accurate quote, and it begins with the care taken in writing the brief itself. Frequently Asked Questions

Skincare line costs
Can a manufacturer quote accurately from a product idea alone?

No. An idea alone leaves the manufacturer guessing about the product type, the units per product, the packaging, the target retail price, and the timeline, and every one of those details moves the price. A brief that answers those points lets the manufacturer quote the work you actually want, on a basis you can compare.

Why does my target retail price matter to a manufacturer?

Your target retail price tells the manufacturer what kind of product, pack, and presentation the market must support. Without it, the manufacturer cannot tell whether a proposed fill, container, or finish fits the price you need to charge, and the quote may describe a product you cannot sell at the price you intended.
