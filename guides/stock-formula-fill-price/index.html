How stock-formula fill pricing works

When development is already done, the fill price becomes the heart of the per-unit charge. This guide explains what that price covers, what sits outside it, and how to compare it fairly.

Updated 28 October 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges below as starting points for your own quotes.

What the fill price is

The fill price is the per-unit charge for the product itself, as supplied in its container. With a stock formula, the formula already exists, so the founder is paying for the product that goes into each unit rather than for the work of creating the formula. That is the core idea behind stock-formula fill pricing in the skincare line cost guide: development is already done, and the fill price is the main per-unit charge that remains. The published band is fill at $1.50–$12.00 per unit (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote), by product type. The band is a starting point for judging quotes, not a price from any manufacturer.

Fill pricing is charged per unit, which means the unit count for each product multiplies the fill line directly. A larger run means more units to fill, and a smaller run means fewer, subject to the minimum that sets the floor for the run. The fill price is therefore inseparable from the units decision: a founder cannot judge a fill price without knowing how many units it will be applied to, and cannot judge a unit count without knowing what each unit will cost to fill. The two decisions belong together, and quotes should be requested with both stated plainly — the product, and the units of that product to be filled.

It is worth pausing on the word stock, because it carries the whole logic of the price. Stock means the formula is an existing one, already developed and already in production for the manufacturer's programme. The founder selects from what exists, applies branding, and orders a run. The fill price reflects that position: the founder is buying product that already has a production history, rather than funding the creation of a new formula. That distinction is what separates fill pricing from development pricing, and it is why the fill price can be stated per unit while development work is stated per formula.

What fill pricing covers

At its simplest, the fill price covers the formulated product supplied for each unit in the run. The product is made to the stock formula, in the format the founder has selected, and supplied ready for the packaging and labelling lines that follow. The exact boundary of what a given manufacturer's fill price includes — for example, how filling itself is handled within the stated price — varies by manufacturer, and the founder should confirm the boundary in writing rather than assuming it. The band does not settle the boundary for any individual manufacturer, because each manufacturer prices differently. What the band does is give the founder a frame for the conversation: this is the per-unit product line, and everything else must be named separately.

The confirmation habit matters because fill prices that look comparable can hide different inclusions. One manufacturer may state a fill price that includes a handling element another bills separately, and the totals will differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the product itself. Asking what the fill price includes, in plain terms, removes that ambiguity before quotes are compared. The question is not confrontational; it is the normal work of reading a quote. Manufacturers who itemise clearly make the question easy to answer, and founders who ask it early avoid the harder conversation that follows when an assumed inclusion turns out to be a separate line.

Confirm the fill boundary in writing. Ask what the stated fill price includes and what is billed separately, so the per-unit product line can be compared fairly across manufacturers.

What sits outside the fill price

The fill price is one line among several, and the lines outside it are where founders most often under-budget. Packaging is billed per unit on top of the fill price: the container, the closure, and any outer carton are separate lines, each with its own handling. Labels are likewise separate: the printed label, its application, and any inserts or seals sit outside the fill price as their own lines. Testing is a further line whose billing varies — whether testing is included in a quoted price or billed separately must be confirmed, because the fill band does not answer that question. None of those lines is hidden by a clear quote, but each must be named to be budgeted.

The separation is useful once it is understood. Because packaging, labels, and testing are stated separately, the founder can see which line is moving the total and can change that line deliberately — choosing a stock container rather than a distinctive one, simplifying a label specification, or confirming the testing scope — rather than simply accepting or rejecting a single total. The fill price, viewed as one bucket among several, becomes a manageable part of the launch. Viewed as the whole launch cost, it becomes a misleading one, because the lines outside it will still need funding whether or not they were named in the first conversation.

Freight and business setup sit further outside the fill price, in the same way. Freight moves finished goods to the founder, and setup lines such as photography and filings belong to the founder's own budget rather than to the manufacturer's product price. Those lines are not part of fill pricing at all, and they should be budgeted on their own terms alongside the production lines. A founder who holds the fill price, the packaging and label lines, the testing line, and the outside lines as separate budgets will understand a quote far better than a founder who holds a single total and hopes it covers everything.

Why fill pricing varies by product type

The fill band is stated by product type for a reason: different products differ in ways that affect the per-unit product charge. Format matters — a cream, a cleanser, and a serum are different products to make and to fill, with different ingredients, proportions, and handling. The work involved in filling differs with the format and the container, and the ingredients involved differ with the formula. The band therefore spans a range, and a founder should expect the fill price for a given product to sit somewhere within that range according to its type, rather than expecting every product to carry the same fill price.

The practical consequence is that fill prices should only be compared between the same product type and format. Comparing the fill price of a cleanser with the fill price of a serum tells the founder little, because the products differ in the very respects that move the price. Comparing the fill price of the same cleanser, in the same format and container size, across manufacturers tells the founder a great deal, because the basis is shared and the remaining difference is the price itself. The discipline is the same as for any quote comparison: same products, same units, same packaging, or the comparison misleads. Fill pricing rewards that discipline more than most lines, because the product-type variation is large enough to swamp a careless comparison entirely.

Fill pricing and minimums

Fill pricing meets minimums at the unit count. A minimum is the smallest run a manufacturer will do, usually set per product rather than per brand, and the fill line for a product is the fill price multiplied by the units in that product's run, subject to that minimum. A founder who plans fewer units than the minimum will find the run sized up to the minimum, with the fill line sized accordingly. A founder who plans well above the minimum will find the fill line growing with every additional unit. The minimum is therefore not a separate topic from fill pricing; it is the floor beneath it.

That floor has a planning consequence worth naming. Because each product carries its own minimum, a launch with several products carries several floors, and the fill lines for the launch as a whole are the sum of the per-product runs. Adding a product adds a new minimum and a new fill line, even if the product is a close variant of one already in the lineup. Founders who plan lineup size and units together — asking, for each product, how many units will be filled and whether that count sits sensibly against the minimum — will find their fill budget easier to read than founders who plan the lineup first and discover the minimums afterwards. For the background on how minimums work, read How to choose a skincare manufacturer, which covers the questions that reveal how a manufacturer handles minimums, samples, and commercial clarity.

How to compare fill quotes

Comparing fill quotes well means holding the basis steady and the inclusions visible. Request every quote for the same product type and format, the same units per product, and the same container, and ask each manufacturer to state the fill price, what it includes, and what is billed separately. With that basis shared, the fill band becomes a useful sense-check: a quoted fill price far outside the band prompts a question about what differs — product type, format, inclusions, or run size — and the answer will usually be visible in the quote itself. A quoted fill price inside the band is not automatically a good price, but it is a price that sits within the industry-wide framing the band describes.

The questions that complete the comparison are short and should be asked of every manufacturer in the same words. What does the fill price include? What packaging and label lines sit outside it, and how are they charged? How is testing treated, and is the test list stated in writing? What is the minimum for this product, and how does the stated unit count sit against it? A founder who asks those questions of each manufacturer will receive quotes that can be compared bucket by bucket, and will understand the fill price for what it is: the per-unit charge for an existing formula, applied to a stated run, with everything outside it named separately. That understanding is the whole point of learning how fill pricing works, and it is what turns a quoted figure into a decision the founder can defend.

Frequently Asked Questions

Stock-formula fill pricing
Does the fill price include packaging and labels?

Usually it does not. Packaging and labels are separate per-unit lines billed on top of the fill price, so confirm on every quote which lines are included and which are separate.

Why does fill pricing vary by product type?

Fill pricing varies by product type because different products differ in format, ingredients, and the work involved in filling. Compare fill prices only between the same product type and format.
