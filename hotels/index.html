Private-label amenities for hotels & resorts

A guest remembers the shower. The scent in the room, the lotion by the sink, the product they take home in their bag — amenities are the most-touched brand surface a hotel owns. A private-label amenity program puts your property's name on those moments instead of a generic supplier's. This page covers how hotel programs work, how to think about volumes across rooms and seasons, and the questions that keep an amenity program from becoming a storage problem.

Updated 28 October 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges and descriptions below as starting points for your own quotes.

What a hotel program looks like

A hotel amenity program is usually a coordinated set: shampoo, conditioner, body wash, body lotion, and often hand soap — in guest-room sizes, with your property's branding, and frequently a retail size of the same products for the gift shop or spa. The set shares a scent identity, so the room, the spa, and the shop smell like the same place. Larger properties add turndown items, spa products for the wellness floor, or seasonal variations. The program is as much a brand decision as a purchasing one: the amenity is the property, experienced at skin level.

The other half of the program is the take-home effect. Guests who like the in-room products buy the retail sizes — at the shop, from the spa, or from a card in the room. That retail tail is where an amenity program pays for itself beyond the guest-experience value, and it is worth designing for from the start rather than discovering afterward.

Thinking in rooms, not units

Hotel volumes are driven by occupancy, not by individual purchase decisions: rooms multiplied by occupancy multiplied by stay length, with seasonal peaks and troughs. That makes forecasting unusually tractable — you can estimate usage from numbers you already track — but it also makes over-ordering easy, because a "small" per-room quantity becomes thousands of units at property scale. Size the first order to a realistic occupancy window, plan replenishment around your seasons, and agree storage and delivery arrangements before the first pallet arrives. Dispenser systems vs. individual bottles change the math significantly: dispensers cut per-room cost and waste, bottles maximize the take-home effect. Decide which you value before you quote.

Forecast from occupancy, not optimism. Rooms × occupancy × average stay gives you a defensible usage number. Order to that number for one season, then reorder on actual usage — the second order is always smarter than the first.

The five steps, in general terms

Every manufacturer runs its own version of this process, but the shape is consistent across the industry. Knowing the steps in advance lets you spot where a quote ends and where extra work — and extra cost — begins.

  1. Choose your path. Stock (private-label) formulas that already exist, or a custom formula developed to your specification. Stock is faster and cheaper to start; custom gives you a formula nobody else sells. Our guide to private label vs. custom formulation compares the two paths in detail.
  2. Evaluate samples. Test the product the way your clients will use it — on skin, over days, in your treatment room or at home. One sample round rarely settles it; budget time for a second round on anything custom.
  3. Branding and packaging. Your label, your container, your outer packaging. The brand owner is generally responsible for label content and regulatory compliance, so confirm early who supplies the artwork, who checks the ingredient list, and what the label minimums are.
  4. Testing and compliance checks. Stability, preservative-effectiveness, and safety testing expectations depend on the product type and where you sell. Ask what testing is included in the quote and what is billed separately — and read our MoCRA explainer for the brand-owner responsibilities that sit with you either way.
  5. First production run and reorder. Production runs to an agreed quantity, ships to you, and the relationship continues into reorders — which usually carry the same minimums, so plan the second run before the first sells out.

A sensible starter lineup

The core five — shampoo, conditioner, body wash, body lotion, hand soap — in one scent identity, in guest-room format, with retail sizes of the two best-loved products for the shop. Properties with a spa add the spa's treatment products as a second set under the same identity. Resist launching with multiple scent families: one identity, done well, is what guests remember and ask for by name.

Frequently Asked Questions

Private label for hotels
How do hotels estimate amenity quantities?

From occupancy: rooms × occupancy rate × average stay length, over the ordering window. That gives a defensible usage estimate. Order one season at a time and reorder on actual usage — the second order is always more accurate than the first.

Dispensers or individual bottles?

Dispensers cut per-room cost and waste; individual bottles maximize the take-home and retail effect. Many properties use dispensers in rooms and bottles in suites and the shop. Decide which outcome you value before requesting quotes, because the formats price differently.

Can multiple properties share one amenity program?

Often yes — a shared scent identity and label system across sister properties usually improves pricing and consistency. Ask about multi-property programs and whether each property can still carry its own name on the label.

What about the retail sizes guests buy?

Plan them from the start: the two products guests ask about most, in retail sizes, sold in the shop or spa. The retail tail is where an amenity program earns beyond the guest-experience value, and it needs its own minimums and label planning.
