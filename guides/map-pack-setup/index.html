What a Map Pack setup actually involves

When a med spa or salon owner goes looking for local SEO help, the Map Pack is usually what they are being sold — the box of three local businesses Google shows for "near me" searches. A "setup" sounds technical. Most of it is not: it is claiming, correcting, and completing your own Google Business Profile, in the right order. This guide walks through what a real setup includes, what to check before paying anyone, and the traps — including one that arrived in our own inbox this week, price list attached.

What is a Map Pack setup, in short?

A Map Pack setup is the work of claiming your Google Business Profile, putting it in the right category, and making its name, address, phone, and hours match reality everywhere your business is listed — then keeping reviews answered and the details current. It is mostly checking and correcting, not software and not secrets. If a pitch skips those basics and leads with rankings, it is not selling a setup.

The order matters more than the volume of work. An unclaimed profile, a duplicate listing, or hours that contradict your own website will undercut everything done afterwards. That is why this guide spends as much time on what to check first as on the setup itself — and why the traps section exists. Businesses are being sold ranking schemes, in writing, at menu prices, while the basics on their own profiles sit undone.

The setup is the boring part. Claiming the profile, fixing the hours, answering the reviews. Anyone selling you something more exciting than that — before those are done — is selling the exciting thing for their benefit, not yours.

What the Map Pack is

Search Google for a service plus a place — "med spa near me", "hair salon" plus your city — and the results page usually leads with a map and three businesses. That box is the Map Pack. It sits above the ordinary website results, and for a local business it is often the first thing a customer sees: name, rating, hours, phone, and a button to call or get directions.

The detail that drives everything else in this guide: the Map Pack is built from Google Business Profile data, not from your website. A salon with a plain website and a complete, claimed, consistent profile can take one of the three spots; a salon with an expensive website and an unclaimed profile showing the wrong hours is competing with one hand tied. Your website still matters — it is where the customer lands next — but the box itself answers to the profile.

What a real setup actually includes

Six pieces of work, all unglamorous. We know because this is the work we do on our own client's presence. 1. Claim and verify the profile — and know who owns it

Claiming means proving to Google that the business is yours and taking ownership of the listing. The trap is not an unclaimed profile; it is a claimed one — claimed by a former marketing vendor, an ex-employee, or an agency login nobody at the business can open. A setup starts by putting the owner login in the business's own hands and removing access that should not be there.

2. The primary category

Every profile has one primary category, and it drives which searches the business can appear in. Renaud's is Manufacturer — right for a company that makes skincare, wrong for a business that sells treatments. A med spa's category choice should be the thing customers actually search for; the individual treatments belong in the services list, not in a pile of extra categories.

3. Name, address, phone — and hours that match reality

The same business name, the same address, and one phone number, written the same way on the profile and everywhere else the business appears. For Renaud that number is (970) 667-1433, and it has to read identically on every listing. Hours are where this quietly breaks. A Colorado manufacturer we work with has a Google listing that shows Monday to Friday, 8 AM–4 PM. Other listings for the same business show 9–5. Both cannot be right — and until the business confirms which hours it actually keeps, that mismatch is a more urgent fix than anything to do with rankings. We could have quietly picked one set of hours and made every listing agree; we didn't, because only the business can say which hours it keeps, and making listings consistent with a guess is how a spa ends up "open" at hours it is closed. The check found the problem. The correction waits on the owner's word — which is the correct order, and the whole point of checking first. If your own listings disagree about when you are open, that is your first job, whoever does it.

4. Services, photos, and attributes, filled in on the profile itself

The treatment menu belongs on the profile as services, with the names customers use. Photos should be the real space, the real rooms, the real team — the profile is where a customer decides whether the business looks like the kind of place they trust. Attributes (the factual labels Google offers a business) should be set where they are true, and left alone where they are not.

5. A review routine: ask, and answer every one

A setup includes the habit, not just the profile fields: a consistent moment to ask a happy client for a review — the aftercare recap at the end of a visit works, for the same reasons it works for selling skincare without being pushy — and a reply to every review that comes in, the bad ones included. An unanswered one-star review tells the next customer more than the review itself does.

6. Citation cleanup

The same name, address, phone, and hours, corrected on the other places that already list the business: Apple Maps, Bing, Yelp, and the directories that picked the business up on their own. Duplicates get merged or removed. This is slow, manual work, and it is the part of a "setup" most often skipped in favour of something that sounds better in a pitch.

The traps: what the sales pitches look like

The clearest way to show the first trap is to describe a real one. We sent an unpaid editorial guest-post pitch on 31 October 2026. On 7 November 2026, a guest-post network that answered our pitch replied — and instead of an editorial answer, sent a price list: guest posts and link insertions across a portfolio of websites the network owns or controls, priced from $10 to $100 per post depending on the site, with payment taken up front and do-follow backlinks included. And the detail that gives the whole thing away: the paid posts would not be marked as sponsored, so nothing would tell a reader the placement was bought.

That last detail is the whole scheme in one sentence: the link is paid for, it is placed to look editorial, and it is deliberately not labelled as paid. That is not a Map Pack setup, or any kind of setup — it is a link scheme with a rate card, sold by the post or in bulk at a discount. We do not pay for postings, and neither should you: it does nothing for an unclaimed profile, a duplicate listing, or hours that say you close at four when you close at five.

Three more traps, in the order we see them. Guaranteed rankings: no vendor controls Google's results, so a guaranteed top spot is a promise about something the seller does not own. Secret methods: the real method is the six-part list above; if a pitch cannot describe the work in plain terms, there is usually no work. Accounts you do not own: a vendor who sets up your profile, reviews, and ads under their own logins has not built your presence — they have built a subscription, and when the contract ends you start again from zero. The check-first list catches all three before any money moves.

Frequently Asked Questions

Map Pack setup for med spas and salons
What is the Map Pack?

The Map Pack is the box of three local business results Google shows near the top of the page for searches like med spa near me. It is drawn from Google Business Profiles, not from websites, so a business with a weak website can still appear there if its profile is claimed, complete, and consistent.

What does a Map Pack setup actually include?

Claiming and verifying the Google Business Profile under the owner login, choosing the right primary category, making the name, address, phone, and hours identical everywhere the business is listed, filling in services, photos, and attributes, setting up a routine for asking for and answering reviews, and cleaning up the same details on Apple Maps, Bing, Yelp, and other directories.

What should I check before paying for local SEO?

Check four things first: that you own the profile login yourself, that there is no duplicate listing, that the hours and phone on the profile match the business as it actually runs, and that no past vendor is still a manager on the account. If any of those fail, fix them before paying for anything else.

Can a vendor guarantee the top Map Pack spot?

No. The vendor does not control Google results, so a guaranteed top spot is a promise about something the seller cannot deliver on demand. Treat a guarantee as a reason to walk away, and ask instead what work will be done, in which accounts, and who owns those accounts when the contract ends.

Should I pay for guest posts or links to improve my local ranking?

That is buying links, not a Map Pack setup. The menus for it are explicit: priced per post, paid up front, with do-follow links and, in the offer we received, no sponsored tag to disclose the payment. It does nothing to fix an unclaimed profile or wrong hours, which are the things a setup is actually for.
