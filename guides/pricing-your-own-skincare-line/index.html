How to price your own skincare line?

Most spa owners set house-line prices by feel — a little under the brand they already stock, a little over what the product cost to make. That is why so many own-label lines barely break even. Pricing is arithmetic: three numbers, one formula, and a check against the shelf.

How do I price my own skincare line?

Start with your true cost per unit — product, packaging, labels, and freight — then pick a target gross margin and work backwards: retail price equals cost divided by one minus your margin. A product costing $12 to make retails around $24 at a 50% gross margin.

That formula is the whole method; the rest of this guide is about its inputs. Cost is easy to understate — freight and labels hide in other budgets. Margin is easy to set by habit — whatever the brand on your shelf charges is not your margin. And the shelf is the check most founders skip: clients already pay known prices for every product type, and your line should be priced against those.

Price from margin, not from markup. A 200% markup and a 66% margin describe the same price — but only the margin tells you what is left after the sale. Set the margin first, then let the price follow.

The three numbers you need first

Before any formula, gather three numbers — if any one is a guess, the price is a guess too.

1. True cost per unit

Your cost per unit is everything in one finished, labeled product on your shelf: fill price, container and cap or pump, printed label, and your share of freight. The skincare line cost guide breaks each into industry ranges, and the private-label margin calculator adds them up. The usual mistake is counting only the fill price — packaging and labels are often 20 to 40 percent of the per-unit cost on a small first run.

2. Target gross margin

Gross margin is the share of the retail price left after the product cost — the money that pays for rent, staff, marketing, and profit. A 40 to 60 percent gross margin is the commonly cited target for beauty brands (BlankaBrand, 2026). Fifty percent is a practical starting point for a house line sold in your own spa. Set the target before you set the price — a margin chosen after the price is just a description of whatever happened.

3. What the shelf already charges

Clients already have a price in mind for every product type, set by the brands on your shelf and every other spa they visit. Our 2026 med spa retail report sampled 14 public spa menus: cleansers $38 to $39, serums $80 to $190, moisturizers $85 to $185, SPF $46 to $48. Those are the numbers your price will be judged against, silently, at the shelf. A serum at $30 next to $120 serums does not look like a bargain; it looks like a different, lesser product.

Markup vs. margin — the two ways to quote price

Markup and margin describe the same price from opposite directions, and mixing them up is the most common pricing error in the industry. Markup is the price over cost: a product that costs $12 and sells for $24 carries a 100% markup. Margin is the profit share of the price: that $24 sale carries a 50% margin, because $12 of it is profit.

Published guidance uses both. Private Label Skincare AU suggests a wholesale markup of 100 to 150 percent and a retail markup of 200 to 300 percent for private-label skincare (checked 31 October 2026) — a $12 product wholesaling at $24 to $30 and retailing at $36 to $48. BlankaBrand's pricing guide warns against pricing from markup multiples alone: set the price from a target margin instead. The two agree when the numbers are right — a 200% markup is a 66.7% margin — but margin is the one that tells you whether the business works, so use it as the primary input and markup as a cross-check.

Read the table across: pick your cost, pick your margin, and the price is fixed. Round to a clean number — $24 becomes $25 — and check it against the shelf ranges above. If it lands far below comparable products, the margin is probably too low, not the market too high.

A worked example: three products

Suppose a med spa launches three stock-formula products at a 60% gross margin. The figures are illustrative, from the cost ranges in the cost guide: a cleanser costing $6.00 retails at $15.00, a serum costing $12.00 at $30.00, a moisturizer costing $10.00 at $25.00.

At 250 units of each, the three products return $3,750 in gross margin on the cleanser run, $4,500 on the serum, and $3,750 on the moisturizer — $12,000 across the launch before rent, staff, and marketing. The margin calculator runs the same math with your own costs, plus total profit and break-even units: its worked example, cost $8 and retail $24 at 250 units, returns $16.00 margin per unit, 66.7%, and $3,850 total after $150 of fees.

Now the shelf check. A serum at $30 sits well below the $80 to $190 serums the report sampled from real spa menus. That does not mean $30 is wrong — a simpler serum can profitably sell for less — but the price should be a deliberate choice. If your serum is comparable to the $120 serums on the shelf, price it near them and keep the extra margin. The formula gives you a floor; the shelf gives you the range.

Price against the shelf, not just the cost

Cost-plus pricing has one blind spot: it assumes clients buy on cost, and they do not. They buy on what the product does for them and what comparable products cost. A post-peel serum prescribed as aftercare is compared with the other post-peel serums the client has seen, not with your cost sheet. Price for the job it does, and let the margin formula confirm the price works.

There is one more reason the shelf matters: your own-label price is the one price no competitor can undercut on Google. A branded product you stock can be found cheaper online by any client with a phone; your house line cannot. That advantage is wasted if you price the line as though it were competing with Amazon. The private label vs. reselling margins guide compares the two models on exactly this point.

Leave room for discounts and bundles

Every spa runs promotions eventually, and the price you set today decides whether they make money or quietly lose it. Build the discount into the price first. A 20% sale on a product priced at a 50% margin still leaves roughly 37.5% margin, because the discount comes off the price, not the profit; at a 40% margin it leaves only 25%. Private Label Skincare AU makes the same point: leave wiggle room for discounts, or the first sale you run will be the one that costs you.

Bundles are the better discount. A three-product regimen sold as a set at 10% off moves three units in one sale, introduces clients to the full routine, and keeps more margin per transaction than discounting a single bestseller. The retail shelf ROI calculator shows how sell-through changes the return on a shelf: a bundle that sells in weeks beats a single product that sits for months.

If you wholesale your line to other spas

Some house lines grow past one shelf. If other spas ask to stock your products, you are a wholesaler as well as a retailer. The commonly suggested wholesale markup is 100 to 150 percent over cost (Private Label Skincare AU, checked 31 October 2026): a product costing $12 wholesales at $24 to $30, and the buying spa retails it at its own margin. Pravada notes that private-label products carry no set price or minimum advertised price — you set the price, and the stockist sets theirs.

Keep your direct retail price above your wholesale price with room to spare. Retail a serum at $30 while wholesaling it at $30, and stockists cannot make money; retail at $48 and wholesale at $30, and the stockist has a workable margin while your clients pay a fair shelf price. Decide the wholesale price before you launch, even with no stockists yet — repricing a product already on your shelf is harder than pricing it right the first time.

Five pricing mistakes

  • Pricing from cost alone. Cost sets the floor, not the price. A serum that costs $12 and sells for $24 is fine arithmetic — until the shelf shows comparable serums at $120.
  • Copying a big brand's price. A national brand's price reflects its marketing budget, distribution, and cost base — none of which are yours.
  • Forgetting the small costs. Freight, payment processing, and label reprints are small per unit and large in total. Leave them out and your real margin is lower than the one you think you have.
  • Discounting to cost to move slow stock. A product that does not sell at full price usually has a product problem, not a price problem. Discounting it to cost teaches clients to wait for the next discount.
  • Changing prices often. Clients notice, and frequent changes read as uncertainty. Set the price, check it against the shelf, and leave it alone for at least one full sell-through cycle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Pricing your skincare line
What markup should I put on my own skincare line?

A retail markup of 200 to 300 percent is commonly suggested for private-label skincare sold direct to clients (Private Label Skincare AU, checked 31 October 2026), which is roughly the same as a 50 to 60 percent gross margin. Start from your true cost per unit and work backwards from your target margin, not from a markup multiple alone.

How do I calculate my retail price from cost?

Divide your cost per unit by one minus your target margin: retail price equals cost divided by (1 minus target margin). A product costing $12 with a 50 percent target margin retails at $24. Include the fill price, packaging, labels, and freight in the cost — the margin calculator on this site does the same math.

Should my house line cost less than the brands I already stock?

Not necessarily. Our 2026 report sampled 14 public spa menus and found serums at $80 to $190 and moisturizers at $85 to $185. Price against what clients already pay for comparable products, not below it — a lower price signals lower quality, and your own-label price is the one no competitor can undercut online.

What margin should a med spa target on retail skincare?

A 40 to 60 percent gross margin is the commonly cited target for beauty brands (BlankaBrand, 2026). Fifty percent is a practical starting point for a house line sold in your own spa. Wholesaling your line to other stockists usually runs at a lower margin, with a 100 to 150 percent markup over cost.

How do I offer discounts without losing money?

Build the discount into the price before you set it. A 20 percent sale on a product priced at a 50 percent margin still leaves roughly 37.5 percent margin. Discount a regimen bundle rather than your single bestseller, and never discount below your true cost per unit.
