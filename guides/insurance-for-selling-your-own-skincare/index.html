Do I need insurance to sell my own skincare line?

Insurance is the launch cost nobody budgets for — until the first customer reaction, the first market that asks for a certificate, or the first retailer that will not stock a line without one. It is also one of the cheapest parts of a launch, and the one that protects everything else.

Do I need insurance to sell my own skincare line?

Yes, in practice — product liability insurance is the baseline cover for anyone selling skincare, even at a few thousand dollars a year. No US law requires it for most sellers, but marketplaces, retailers, and events commonly do: Amazon requires it once sales pass $10,000 a month. A small policy commonly costs a few hundred dollars a year.

The short answer is "yes" for a simple reason: the moment your product touches someone else's skin, you are legally responsible for what happens after the sale. That responsibility does not scale down with revenue — a brand selling $1,000 a year faces the same kind of claim as one selling $100,000, and the claim is measured in legal fees and medical bills, not in your sales. This guide covers what the cover is, what it costs, where it is required, and how to get it.

Your homeowners policy does not cover this. A standard homeowners policy excludes business liability and caps business property at about $2,500 on premises. A customer injury claim tied to your product is a business claim — without a separate policy, it lands on your personal savings.

Why a small brand needs it

Most solo makers assume they are too small to insure, so they never apply. In practice, beauty and cosmetics insurance is a routine market, and small-batch cosmetics insurance exists specifically for makers at this stage. Carriers will write a policy even when revenue is only a few thousand dollars a year — being solo or home-based does not make you uninsurable. The underwriter prices on what you make and sell, not on whether you work from a kitchen table or a rented lab.

The legal reason is strict liability: under it, a defective cosmetic that injures a customer creates the same legal claim whether it sold on Etsy, at a market, or through your own site. It does not matter that you meant well, that the formula was natural, or that a thousand other customers had no reaction. Even simple products can cause real problems — a cinnamon oil that irritates sensitive skin, a fragrance that triggers an allergy — and the claim that follows is for medical bills, legal fees, and a settlement, whether or not you are found at fault. Product liability coverage is the baseline any maker with physical product risk should carry.

What the cover actually covers

Three kinds of cover matter for a skincare line, and one policy usually handles the first two together:

Cover What it pays for Example
Product liability Injuries, allergic reactions, or property damage caused by your products A customer says your serum caused a rash — legal fees, settlements, damages
General liability Third-party injuries or property damage around your business, not from the product Someone trips at your booth during a market — their medical bills
Professional liability Claims from hands-on services you provide, not what you sell You teach a soap-making class and a student has an unexpected reaction

A general liability policy already includes product coverage by default, so one policy usually handles both the trip-and-fall at a market booth and the skin reaction to your face cream. Professional liability is separate and only matters if you also sell advice or services alongside the products — recommendations for skin types, classes, or consultations. If you sell only products, the first two are the cover you need. Tell the broker everything you sell, including anything with CBD, SPF, or high concentrations of essential oils: those are considered higher risk and need additional underwriting, and disclosing them upfront is what keeps the policy valid.

What it costs

At low revenue, a general liability policy that includes product coverage commonly runs a few hundred dollars a year. CoverWatch gives the example of a soap maker selling $8,000 a year of cold-process bars at farmers markets and on Etsy paying a few hundred dollars annually for a $1M/$2M policy — $1 million per occurrence, $2 million aggregate. A small home-based business owners policy runs more once property is added. Insurance Canopy advertises beauty product liability cover from $99 a month, which is a different tier of the same market — the price depends on what you make and sell, your annual revenue, and your sales channels.

The number that matters is the limit, not the premium. A $1M/$2M policy is the standard ask: $1 million per claim, $2 million total across the policy year. Retailers and marketplaces that require insurance usually name that limit in their requirements, so buying below it saves little and can still leave you unable to sell where you want to. Get quotes from two or three brokers that write small-batch and indie cosmetics — the market is competitive at this size, and the spread between quotes is often larger than the premium itself. Ask every broker the same questions — the limit, the deductible, and what is excluded — so the quotes compare like for like, and keep them on file beside the launch budget.

Getting covered

Apply through a broker or carrier that writes small-batch and indie cosmetics, and share your product list, ingredients, annual revenue, and sales channels. A one-person brand can usually bind a product liability policy within a few days. Clean, consistent formulas and clear labels make the application simpler — the underwriter is reading the same label your customers read, and a label that passes the MoCRA checklist is evidence of a careful maker.

If you use a contract manufacturer, the underwriter will ask about the manufacturer's liability and whether the manufacturer names you on their policy, which can lower your own rate. But the manufacturer's policy is not yours: the brand owner is the one customers sue, not the factory. Your policy answers for your brand; theirs answers for their factory. Keep the certificate of insurance where you can send it the same day a retailer asks — a brand that can produce proof of cover immediately looks like a professional partner, not a risk. The manufacturer quote brief guide lists what to send a manufacturer, and asking about their liability cover belongs in the same conversation.

Where it is required

There is no nationwide mandate requiring insurance to sell skincare products, but the places you sell through increasingly set their own rules. Amazon requires liability insurance once sales exceed $10,000 per month. Etsy does not require it, but strongly recommends it. Shopify does not require it. State or city requirements vary. And many major online marketplaces, retailers, and events list insurance as a prerequisite to sell — a retailer that stocks your line will usually ask for the certificate before the first order, not after a claim.

Insurance is also separate from MoCRA. The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act requires facility registration, product listing, safety substantiation, and adverse-event reporting — it does not require insurance, and insurance does not substitute for any of it. The $1M small-business exemption covers facility registration and product listing, not liability. The brand-owner duties guide covers what MoCRA actually requires of you, and the MoCRA rules guide for spas covers the spa side. Treat insurance and MoCRA as two separate checklists that both need completing before launch — one protects the business, the other keeps the product legal.

When the cover is in place, the launch can start on a sound footing: a compliant label, a registered facility, a listed product, and a policy that answers if a customer is hurt. Frequently Asked Questions

Insurance for your skincare line
Do I need insurance to sell skincare from home?

Yes, in practice. Even if you mix scrubs in your kitchen, once your product touches someone else's skin you are legally liable for what happens after the sale. A standard homeowners policy excludes business liability and caps business property at about $2,500 on premises, so it does not cover a product business.

What does product liability insurance cover?

Injuries, allergic reactions, or property damage caused by your products — a customer says your serum caused a rash, and the policy helps pay legal fees, medical bills, settlements, or damages. It covers the claim whether or not you are found at fault. General liability covers injuries around your business, such as a trip at a market booth.

How much does skincare product liability insurance cost?

At low revenue, a general liability policy that includes product coverage commonly runs a few hundred dollars a year — CoverWatch gives the example of a soap maker selling $8,000 a year paying a few hundred dollars annually for a $1M/$2M policy. The price depends on what you make and sell, your revenue, and your sales channels, not on whether you have a storefront.

Will my homeowners insurance cover my skincare business?

No. A standard homeowners policy excludes business liability through the business pursuits exclusion and caps business property at about $2,500 on premises, according to the Insurance Information Institute. A customer injury claim tied to your product is a business claim, and it lands on your personal savings without a separate policy.

Do I need insurance if I use a contract manufacturer?

Yes — the brand owner is the one customers sue, not the factory. When you apply, the underwriter will ask about your contract manufacturer's liability and whether the manufacturer names you on their policy, which can lower your own rate. Your policy and the manufacturer's policy are separate, and yours is the one that answers for your brand.
