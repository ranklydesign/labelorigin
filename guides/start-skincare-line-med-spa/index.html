How to start your own skincare line as a med spa

A med spa already sells skincare. Every treatment ends with a recommendation, and every recommendation is a product your client will buy somewhere. A private-label line turns that recommendation into a product with your name on it, sold as the aftercare to the treatments you already perform. This guide walks through the launch in the order that works: products first, path second, samples third, money fourth.

Updated 6 November 2026 • General educational information for med spa owners. Every practice and every manufacturer works a little differently — use this guide as a starting point, not a promise.

How do you start your own skincare line as a med spa?

Start from the treatment menu: pick three to five products tied to your most-booked services — a gentle cleanser and barrier moisturizer for post-procedure care, a serum, sun protection, and one targeted product. Request a sample kit, confirm the per-SKU minimums in writing, then run the first order. That regimen, not a twenty-product catalog, is the shape to launch.

The most-booked services on your menu decide the line. A med-spa line is usually three to five products tied to the treatments clients already come in for: a gentle cleanser and barrier moisturizer for post-procedure care, a daily serum for maintenance between visits, sun protection, and one targeted product matched to your most popular service. That is the shape manufacturers who work with med spas recommend — a curated regimen of three to five SKUs, not a twenty-product catalog.

Keep the first edit tight. Trade guidance for spas launching a private-label line is to start with one to three products that complement existing services, then expand only where clients ask for more. Every additional scent, size, or formula is another inventory decision, another minimum, and another product your team has to learn to recommend. A short line your providers can explain in one sentence outsells a long line nobody can describe.

Sell it as aftercare first and retail second. The consultation recommends the products, the front desk dispenses them, and the client returns because the routine works. That framing matters: the International SPA Association Foundation’s New Era, New Consumer study found 47 percent of spa participants prefer a hybrid approach that combines professional services with at-home care. Your line is the at-home half of the treatment your client just paid for. The private-label page for med spas shows what a line built this way looks like, product by product.

Aftercare first, retail second. A line that reads as a sales display gets ignored. A line prescribed as the aftercare to a treatment the client just had gets bought — and rebought.

Choose your path: stock formulas or custom

There are two ways to get a line, and the choice shapes everything downstream: cost, minimums, and timeline.

Stock formulas (private label)

You select formulas that already exist — developed, tested, and already in production — and sell them under your own brand and label. This is the faster route and the lower-minimum route, and it is how most med spas start. The trade-off is that the formula is not exclusive to you: the manufacturer may sell the same base formula to other brands, though your label, packaging, and positioning are yours.

Custom formulation

A formula is developed to your specification — texture, actives, claims, and positioning built for your brand alone. It takes longer, costs more to develop, and usually needs a second sample round and higher minimums. The payoff is a product nobody else sells. Our guide to private label vs. custom formulation compares the two paths in detail.

Most med spas start with stock formulas and add a custom hero product later, once the line is selling and the first reorders are in. Starting custom on every SKU at once is how a launch budget doubles before a single product reaches the shelf.

Samples before anything

Request a sample kit before you commit to any product, any formula, or any manufacturer. Try the formulas in your treatment room, on real post-procedure skin, over days — the way your clients will actually use them. Have your providers evaluate samples specifically on post-procedure suitability: skin recovering from a procedure needs gentler, simpler formulas than daily maintenance products, and a formula that feels fine on healthy skin can sting on compromised skin.

Do not approve a formula from a description or a sales sheet. The sample round is also the moment to judge the manufacturer: how fast samples arrive, whether the formulas match what was promised, and whether the team answers questions straight. A manufacturer that is slow or vague at the sample stage will not get faster after you have paid.

Cost it before you commit

Build the cost sheet before you set a single shelf price. The published first-run bands in our cost guide are roughly $1,500–$10,000 for a stock-formula launch and $5,000–$25,000+ for a custom-formulation launch, and those are general industry patterns, not a quote. Run your own numbers through the launch cost calculator with your products, units, and packaging to see the floor and the ceiling for your line.

Alongside the filled unit, account for freight, testers, bags, staff incentives, and expected discounts — the costs that sit outside the per-unit price and still come out of the margin. Compare quotes like-for-like: same products, same units, same buckets. Treat any quote that will not itemize those buckets as a warning sign, and remember that no figure on a website is a quote from any manufacturer. Only an itemized quote on the same basis is real.

Brand, label, and the law

Your name, your protocols, your packaging. Keep the brand consistent with the practice: the same tone as your consultation room, the same claims your providers are comfortable making, and no borrowed clinical language you cannot support. Clients who trust the clinic trust the products; clients who catch an overstatement stop trusting both.

Get the label reviewed before it is printed. Under MoCRA — the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 — the “responsible person” is the manufacturer, packer, or distributor whose name appears on the product label, and for a private-label brand that is usually you, the brand owner, not the manufacturer that made the product. A manufacturer can do much of the practical work, such as registering its facility and listing products, but the legal duties stay with you. Our MoCRA guide for brand owners explains the duties in plain terms, and the MoCRA checklist turns them into eight items to work through before your first sale.

Train the team, launch as aftercare

A line fails at the front desk more often than it fails in the formula. Before products reach the retail display, train the team on a short recommendation that connects each product to the service the client just had: what they experienced in the treatment room, how the product continues it at home, and what to expect. Practice it until it sounds like advice, not a pitch. Decide who tracks stock and what triggers a reorder, and launch to your existing clients first — the people who already trust you are the cheapest test of whether the line sells.

Plan the launch materials while products are in production: product photography, a one-page aftercare sheet per treatment, and an email to your client list. The launch is not a single day. It is the first month of recommendations, refills, and the first reorder — and the reorder is the real test. A line that sells through its first run and gets reordered is a line. A line that sits on the shelf is inventory.

What to ask a manufacturer before you commit

Give the manufacturer a written summary — product type, intended use, fill size, packaging, quantity per SKU, budget range, desired delivery date — and ask these questions in writing, before you commit:

  • What is the minimum per SKU, and is it per SKU or per order?
  • How does the sample process work, and what does it cost?
  • What testing is included in the quote and what is billed separately — stability, preservative-effectiveness, safety?
  • Who reviews the label, and is regulatory review part of the service?
  • What is the turnaround for stock formulas, and for custom?
  • Do reorder minimums match first-order minimums?
  • What happens if a formula is discontinued?

Ask when the production clock begins and what must happen first: formula approval, artwork approval, and component availability can be separate milestones. Request a schedule that includes sampling, revisions, packaging, and delivery, then plan staff training and launch materials around the confirmed dates.
