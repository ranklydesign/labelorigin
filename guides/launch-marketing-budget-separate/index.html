Keep Marketing Budget Out of Production Math

Making a product and selling a product are different jobs, with different costs, different timing, and different risks. When the money for both jobs is mixed into a single total, neither job can be judged honestly, and the launch can look affordable right up to the moment the goods arrive with no plan and no funds left to reach buyers.

Two separate jobs, two separate budgets

Production math answers a narrow question: what will it cost to turn a formula, packaging, labels, and testing into finished goods in your hands? That question has a clear shape. Each cost sits in a bucket, each bucket can be quoted, and the total can be checked line by line against competing quotes. Launch marketing answers a different question: what will it cost, in money and in time, to make the right buyers aware of the product, to earn their trust, and to turn that awareness into orders? That question has a looser shape, because much of the work is ongoing, much of it is creative, and much of its value arrives slowly rather than in a single delivery. Treating the two questions as if they were one total flattens both of them. Production looks more expensive than it is, marketing looks cheaper than it is, and the founder is left with a single number that explains neither job well.

Separation also protects timing. Production spending tends to arrive in large steps, tied to deposits, production runs, and freight. Marketing spending tends to arrive in smaller, steadier amounts, tied to content, outreach, and follow-up over weeks and months. A single pooled total hides that rhythm. It can make a launch look fully funded at the start, when in truth the early production steps will consume the pool before the steady marketing work has had time to do anything. Separate budgets, each with its own timing, show when money leaves and when it needs to be available. That view matters more than any single total, because a launch rarely fails for lack of a grand total on paper. It fails because the right money is not available at the right moment, for the job that needs doing at that moment.

What launch marketing actually covers

Launch marketing, in the organic sense used throughout this site, covers the work of being found, being understood, and being trusted by buyers who have never heard of the brand. That work includes creating helpful content that answers the questions buyers are already asking, making sure that content can be found through search, building an email list of people who have asked to hear from the brand, sharing useful material through social channels, and building relationships with creators, estheticians, retailers, and other partners who can introduce the product to their own audiences. It also includes the unglamorous follow-up that turns a single mention into a relationship: replying to enquiries, sending samples to the right people with useful context, and keeping in touch without pestering. None of that work is a single purchase. It is a programme of steady effort, and its budget needs to reflect effort as well as outlay, including the founder's own time and the time of anyone helping with writing, photography, or outreach.

The affordability question mixing hides

The question every founder needs to answer before committing is simple to state and hard to face: can I afford to make it, and can I afford to sell it? Those are two questions, not one, and mixing the budgets turns them into a single blurry question that can be answered with false confidence. A founder who has enough money in total to cover production and marketing together may still be unable to afford the launch if production is ordered first and consumes the pool, leaving marketing unfunded. A founder who has enough for production alone may believe the launch is affordable because the production total looks manageable, without ever confronting the marketing cost at all. In both cases the real affordability question is hidden, not answered, and the discovery comes late, when goods are already on their way and the options are to sell without support or to find fresh funds under pressure.

No published band for marketing spend

No published band exists for marketing spend. That statement deserves to be made plainly, because founders reasonably look for a range to plan against, and this guide offers none. The cost guide that this article expands gives published bands for production buckets, such as development, fill, packaging, labels, testing, samples, and freight, each labelled as an estimate based on general industry patterns. It gives no equivalent band for marketing, and this article invents none. Marketing spend varies too widely with the channels chosen, the audience sought, the content created, the help engaged, and the time available to be captured in a single range without misleading the reader. A range that tried to cover every approach, from a founder doing everything personally to a brand engaging outside help across many channels, would be so wide as to be useless, and a narrower range would pretend to a precision that the subject does not support.

When you read launch marketing budgets in the skincare line cost guide, you will see marketing treated as a separate bucket alongside samples, with the clear instruction to set a separate budget and keep it out of the production math. This article follows that instruction and extends it: separate the budget, build it from your own plan, and resist the urge to borrow a figure from elsewhere. The production bands exist to help you judge production quotes. The marketing budget exists to help you judge whether your launch effort is realistic, sustained, and affordable. Those are different jobs, and the tools for each should stay different too.

An organic channel and what it asks of you

Organic channels are the routes to buyers that are earned through useful work rather than bought as placements. They suit a launch with a separate, honest marketing budget because they reward consistency, clarity, and helpfulness over time, and because their costs are mostly time, creation, and relationship-building rather than large single outlays. That does not make them free. Each channel asks for a different kind of effort, and a launch plan that treats them all as the same job will under-resource the ones that matter most to its buyers. The example below describes one organic channel in general terms, so you can weigh what it asks before you commit time and money to it.

Social sharing and creators

Social channels and creators reach buyers through personality, demonstration, and recommendation. A creator who genuinely uses a product and explains why it fits a particular routine can reach people who would never search for it by name. That reach depends on fit and trust: the creator's audience, style, and standards need to match the brand, and the arrangement needs to be honest about what is being shared and why. The cost is mainly time and product: finding the right creators, building relationships, sending samples with useful context, and supporting creators with accurate information. Some creators also charge for their work, and any such arrangement should be agreed in writing before product is sent. For a sense of how creator audiences fit into a launch, see the creators segment page, which describes the launch shape for creator-led brands. Budget for relationship-building as a sustained activity, not a single outreach blast, and judge it by the quality of the fit rather than the size of any single audience.

Keeping the budgets apart in practice

Keeping budgets apart in practice needs only a few habits, applied consistently. Keep a separate sheet or section for marketing, with its own lines for creation, distribution, and relationship work, and its own total. Record production quotes and marketing quotes in their own places, and never move a cost from one to the other to make a total look better. Review each budget on its own timetable: production when quotes arrive and runs are ordered, marketing on a regular rhythm that matches the steady nature of the work. When a decision affects both budgets, record the effect on each one explicitly, so the trade-off is visible to anyone reading the plan, including your future self. And when a cost is genuinely shared, such as photography that serves both the product page and social sharing, split it by a stated rule, record the rule, and apply it consistently rather than counting the whole cost in whichever budget happens to have room.

  • Separate records. Production and marketing each have their own lines, their own totals, and their own review rhythm.
  • Written boundaries. Any cost that could sit in either budget is assigned once, in writing, and kept there.
  • Visible trade-offs. A decision that raises spending in one budget names the effect on the other, so nothing is silently consumed.
  • Regular reviews. Each budget is revisited at natural decision points, before further funds are committed.
  • Time counted honestly. Founder and helper time is part of the marketing budget, even where no invoice is involved.

What to do when money is tight

When money is tight, separate budgets make the honest options clearer, and the honest options are usually about scope and timing rather than about cutting corners on quality. A smaller production run, simpler packaging, or fewer products at launch can bring production within reach without compromising the products that do launch. A narrower audience, fewer channels done well, or a slower timetable that lets organic work build before goods arrive can bring marketing within reach without abandoning the effort altogether. Doing fewer things well is almost always better than doing many things thinly, because thin marketing rarely reaches anyone effectively, while focused marketing, sustained over time, can build a real audience for a focused range. The separate budgets let you test those options on paper, see the effect on each total, and choose deliberately rather than drifting into a launch that is too broad for its funds.

Size the marketing budget from retail, not production

With no published band for marketing spend, the honest anchor is retail revenue, not production cost. Trade guidance puts retail at 15–25% of a med spa's total revenue — if retail is currently near zero, that is revenue left on the table — and the point of a separate marketing budget is to fund the work that earns that share. Two figures from the site's own research set the scale:

  • Retail should be 15–25% of total revenue. Skin Inc, "Medspa Profit Secrets: Unlock Hidden Revenue Streams", updated February 2026.
  • $1.4 million average annual revenue per med spa location. AmSpa State of the Industry Report, 2023 data — see stat 3 in the 2026 med spa retail skincare report. At that scale, a 15–25% retail share is $210,000–$350,000 a year, which is the revenue the marketing budget is built to earn.

That framing also explains why the budget must stay separate: marketing is funded against the retail revenue it produces, not against the production total it sits beside. Owners put the same point in their own words — retail brings in extra income without extra treatment-room hours, and the real margins are in retail, not in the treatment itself. Frequently Asked Questions

Launch marketing budgets
Why should launch marketing have a separate budget?

A separate budget keeps the cost of making products apart from the cost of reaching buyers. Mixing the two hides whether you can afford to make a product, to sell it, or both.

Is there a published band for launch marketing spend?

No published band exists for marketing spend. Set your own budget from the channels you plan to use, the time they will take, and the help you will need, and keep it apart from production totals.
