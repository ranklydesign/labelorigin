Private label vs. reselling a brand: margins for spas

Every spa that retails skincare faces the same choice: resell a brand your clients already know, or sell your own label. The margin looks like the whole story. It is not. Velocity, staff commission, and loyalty decide what the margin is actually worth — and the honest answer is usually both, in different proportions, for different reasons.

Updated 6 November 2026 • General educational information for spa owners. Margin ranges are cited from the industry reporting named; every manufacturer prices differently, and only an itemized quote is a real number.

Does private label or reselling make more money for a spa?

Per unit, your own label usually wins. Resold brands run roughly 30 to 40 percent margin, private label roughly 45 to 55 percent in spa-retail reporting, and up to 60 to 85 percent in a December 2025 analysis. But the number that decides is profit per shelf slot: margin per unit times how often the slot turns in a year.

Reselling a brand means buying a finished, branded product at wholesale and selling it at retail. You are buying the brand’s recognition, its marketing, and its shelf presence — and paying for all three in the wholesale price. Your clients already know the name, your therapists already trust the formulas, and the product practically sells itself. The loyalty it builds belongs to the brand, not to you.

Private label means a manufacturer’s formula under your name, your label, and your price. You are buying the product without the brand’s marketing budget attached — and building the loyalty yourself. Your clients have to learn to trust a name they have never seen before. But when they do, the loyalty belongs to your spa, and nobody else can sell them the same product cheaper online.

What reselling a brand earns

Industry reporting puts the gross margin on established third-party skincare brands at roughly 30 to 40 percent: clinics typically buy these products at approximately 50 to 60 percent of their retail price, and the rest of the retail price covers the brand’s marketing, distribution, and markups. A December 2025 industry analysis of medical offices, med spas, and skincare studios reports the same 30–40 percent band, and puts retail skincare at 6 to 12 percent of revenue for most med spas — with top-performing practices reaching 20 to 40 percent through strategic retail and private-label offerings.

A worked example from spa-retail reporting makes the band concrete. Buy a popular branded moisturizer at wholesale for $30 and retail it for $48, and the gross margin is $18 — 37.5 percent. That is a real margin on a real product. It is also the margin before anything else happens to it.

What private label earns

Reported gross margins for private label run higher — and the honest range is wide, because the sources measure different things. Spa-retail reporting puts private-label serums and moisturizers at roughly 45 to 55 percent gross margin with high repeat purchase rates. The December 2025 industry analysis puts private-label gross margins at 60 to 85 percent, against 30 to 40 percent for retail brands. Vendor copy goes further, quoting markups of 200 to 400 percent — but those are markups on cost, not margins on price, and the two are not the same number: a 200 percent markup means the price is three times the cost, which is a 67 percent gross margin, before anything else is deducted.

The fair way to state it: published reporting puts private-label gross margins somewhere from the mid-40s to the mid-80s percent, against roughly 30 to 40 percent for resold brands. The top of that range assumes low product cost, no distributor in the middle, and full control of the retail price. The bottom assumes realistic costs, packaging, and the testing and labeling work that a first line actually needs. Treat any single number as a claim, and any range with its source as information.

Markup is not margin. A 200% markup means the price is three times the cost — a 67% gross margin, before commission, testers, and shelf costs. When a supplier quotes a margin, ask whether it is markup on cost or margin on price.

The costs margin hides

Gross margin is what you make before the real costs of selling a product in a spa. Take the branded moisturizer above: $30 wholesale, $48 retail, $18 gross. The therapist who sold it typically earns a commission — around 15 percent of the retail price in the worked example, or $7.20 — and the net margin drops to $10.80, or 22.5 percent. Then come the testers you give away, the shelf space the product occupies, the occasional discount, and the products that expire before they sell. A margin quoted without those costs is a gross margin, not a profit.

Private label carries the same hidden costs, plus two of its own: the launch cost of the line itself (the published first-run bands in our cost guide are roughly $1,500–$10,000 for a stock-formula launch and $5,000–$25,000+ for a custom-formulation launch), and the cost of earning trust — samples, staff education, and the first months when clients try a name they do not recognize. Both models pay commission and testers. Only one of them also pays for the brand.

There is one more cost that only reselling carries: online price competition. When you educate a client about a brand’s ingredients and benefits, that client can buy the same product from an online retailer or a big-box store — often at a discount. You did the hard work of the recommendation; someone else made the sale. A private-label product cannot be undercut that way, because nobody else sells it.

Velocity is the other half

A margin is what you make per sale. Velocity is how often you make it. The branded moisturizer in the worked example sells within three weeks because clients recognize the brand and therapists recommend it confidently — reordering every month, turning inventory about twelve times a year. That $10.80 net margin per unit becomes about $129.60 of annual profit per shelf slot.

A private-label product with a higher margin per unit but half the turns can earn less per shelf slot over the year than the branded product it replaced. That is the trap in the margin comparison: the line with the bigger percentage is not automatically the line with the bigger profit. The number that matters is profit per shelf slot — net margin per unit, multiplied by how many times the slot turns in a year, minus what expires unsold.

Control and loyalty

What private label buys that reselling cannot is control. Full pricing control, with no MSRP restrictions and no distributor setting the floor. No one can undercut you online, because the product is yours. And the loyalty it builds belongs to your spa: a client who rebuys your serum rebuys your brand, and comes back to your treatment room for the refill. The December 2025 analysis reports that 92 percent of med spas already retail products, and that the top performers reach 20 to 40 percent of revenue from retail — the practices that treat retail as part of the treatment, not an afterthought, are the ones that get there.

Reselling buys the opposite: credibility you do not have to earn. A recognized brand on the shelf tells a new client your spa stocks professional products, and your therapists can recommend it without first explaining what it is. That credibility is worth real money in the first year of a retail program, and it is the reason most spas keep at least one branded line even after their own label is selling.

The honest answer: both

Keep the branded heroes that bring clients in the door and turn fast — the products your therapists recommend without thinking and your clients ask for by name. Add your own label alongside them, starting with three to five SKUs tied to your most-booked treatments, and let the two lines do different jobs: the brand earns velocity and credibility, your label earns margin and loyalty. The med-spa private-label page shows what the own-label side looks like, and the estheticians’ private-label page covers the same ground for solo studios and day spas.

Do not drop the brand that brings clients in until your label earns its shelf. Watch the turns, not just the margin: if your label is selling through and reordering, expand it. If it is sitting while the branded hero turns, the problem is trust or recommendation, not the formula — and more SKUs will not fix it.

A margin worksheet

Run both models through the same arithmetic, on one shelf slot, over one year. The branded side uses the published worked example: buy at $30, retail at $48, 15 percent staff commission, about twelve turns a year. The private-label side is an illustrative example built from the published ranges — a $48 retail price, a $22 unit cost (a 54 percent gross margin, inside the reported 45–55 percent band), the same 15 percent commission, and six turns a year while the line earns trust:

  • Branded: $48 retail − $30 cost = $18 gross; minus $7.20 commission = $10.80 net per unit; × 12 turns = about $129.60 per shelf slot per year, before testers and expiry.
  • Private label (illustrative): $48 retail − $22 cost = $26 gross; minus $7.20 commission = $18.80 net per unit; × 6 turns = about $112.80 per shelf slot per year, before testers and expiry.

The private-label unit earns nearly twice the net margin — and roughly the same annual profit per slot, because it turns half as often. That is the whole argument in one worksheet: margin per unit is the wrong number to optimize. Optimize profit per shelf slot, and let the turns tell you which line is actually earning. Run your own numbers through the launch cost calculator, the MOQ planner, and the retail margin calculator — which shows what the line earns each month after staff commission — before you commit to either model. Frequently Asked Questions

Private label vs. reselling: margins for spas
What margin do spas make reselling a skincare brand?

Industry reporting puts the gross margin on established third-party brands at roughly 30 to 40 percent — clinics typically buy at 50 to 60 percent of the retail price. After staff commission (often around 15 percent), testers, and shelf costs, the net margin is lower: in a published worked example, a $30 wholesale moisturizer retailed at $48 leaves $10.80 net per unit, or 22.5 percent, after commission.

What margin does a private-label line make?

Published reporting puts private-label gross margins from the mid-40s to the mid-80s percent — spa-retail reporting cites roughly 45 to 55 percent for serums and moisturizers, and a December 2025 industry analysis cites 60 to 85 percent. The range is wide because costs vary; treat any single number as a claim and any range with its source as information.

Is private label always more profitable?

No. A private-label unit usually earns a higher margin, but it turns slower until clients trust the name. Annual profit per shelf slot — net margin per unit multiplied by turns per year, minus what expires unsold — is the number that decides. A branded product turning twelve times a year can out-earn a private-label product turning six times, even at half the margin.

Why do branded products sell faster?

Clients recognize them, trust them, and therapists recommend them confidently without first explaining what the product is. A private-label line has to earn that trust product by product — which is why the first months of samples, staff education, and recommendations matter more than the launch day.

How many SKUs should my own line start with?

Three to five, tied to your most-booked treatments: a cleanser, a barrier moisturizer, a daily serum, sun protection, and one targeted product. Fewer products, each with a clear job, outsell a long line nobody can describe — and every additional SKU is another minimum, another listing, and another product your team has to learn.
