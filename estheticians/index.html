How an esthetician launches a retail skincare line

Disclosure: Renaud Naturals is LabelOrigin's flagship client — Renaud pays LabelOrigin to bring it wholesale buyers. LabelOrigin doesn't sell product; Renaud quotes and fulfills.

Launching a retail skincare line as a solo esthetician starts with what you already do: clients trust your recommendations because your hands are on their skin every appointment. A private-label line puts your name on the products you already recommend — and turns advice you give away free into retail revenue. This page walks the launch, step by step, for solo practitioners and small studios: the five steps, the volumes that work at solo scale, and the questions that keep a first launch from becoming an expensive stockroom. What a solo line looks like

For a solo esthetician, a private-label line is usually two to four products: the cleanser and moisturizer you use in every facial, one treatment product clients ask about, and possibly a body product. That is the whole line, and it is enough — clients buy what you use on them, and a short range means every product earns its shelf space. The branding is yours: your studio name, your label, your story. The formulas come from a manufacturer, either selected from existing stock formulas or developed for you, and the products arrive ready to sell.

The economics are straightforward. You already recommend products after every appointment; today that recommendation sends clients to someone else's brand. Your own line keeps the retail margin in your studio and gives clients a reason to come back to you for refills instead of ordering online. It also deepens the relationship: refills bring clients back to you instead of to an online store.

Why solo volumes need small-batch

A solo practice sells a few units a week, not a few hundred. Standard manufacturing minimums built for national brands would leave you with years of stock and an expiry-date problem. Small-batch programs are built for this scale: runs sized to months of real sales, so your cash stays in your business instead of on a shelf. The trade-off is honest — per-unit costs run higher at small volumes — but a higher per-unit cost on product that sells beats a lower cost on product that expires.

Start with what you already sell through. Look at your last three months: which products do clients rebuy, which do you recommend most, and which run out? Your first line is that list, branded — not a catalog of possibilities.

The five steps for a solo launch

Every manufacturer runs its own version of this process, but the shape is consistent across the industry. Knowing the steps in advance lets you spot where a quote ends and where extra work — and extra cost — begins. The full step-by-step version is our guide to how to start a skincare line.

  1. Choose your path. Stock (private-label) formulas that already exist, or a custom formula developed to your specification. Stock is faster and cheaper to start; custom gives you a formula nobody else sells. Our guide to private label vs. custom formulation compares the two paths in detail.
  2. Evaluate samples. Test the product the way your clients will use it — on skin, over days, in your treatment room or at home. One sample round rarely settles it; budget time for a second round on anything custom.
  3. Branding and packaging. Your label, your container, your outer packaging. The brand owner is generally responsible for label content and regulatory compliance, so confirm early who supplies the artwork, who checks the ingredient list, and what the label minimums are.
  4. Testing and compliance checks. Stability, preservative-effectiveness, and safety testing expectations depend on the product type and where you sell. Ask what testing is included in the quote and what is billed separately — and read our MoCRA explainer for the brand-owner responsibilities that sit with you either way.
  5. First production run and reorder. Production runs to an agreed quantity, ships to you, and the relationship continues into reorders — which usually carry the same minimums, so plan the second run before the first sells out.

A sensible starter lineup

Two products are a legitimate launch: a cleanser and a moisturizer, the pair you use in nearly every service. Add a third — a serum or treatment product — only when clients are already asking for it by name. Resist the full-range launch: every additional SKU multiplies your minimums, your label costs, and your shelf risk. A tight line that sells out is a success; a broad line that gathers dust is a lesson.

Frequently Asked Questions

Launching a retail skincare line
Can a solo esthetician really afford a private-label line?

Often yes, if the program is built for small batches — the whole point of low-minimum programs is runs sized to solo-practice volumes. Published minimums we checked start as low as 6 units per SKU, but the deciding numbers are the per-product minimum and the itemized per-unit cost in your quote — get both in writing before committing.

Should I start with stock formulas or custom formulation?

Stock formulas are the usual starting point for a solo launch: the formula exists, so you pay mainly for product, packaging, and labels. Custom formulation adds development cost and time, and makes sense once you have proven sales and a specific gap in your range.

How many products should my first line have?

Two to four. A cleanser and moisturizer you use daily, plus one treatment product clients ask about, is a complete launch. Every extra SKU multiplies minimums and shelf risk, so expand only when a product has earned its place through client demand.

What happens if a product does not sell?

That is why the first run stays small: unsold stock is the main risk of a first launch. Size runs to a few months of realistic sales, test in the treatment room first, and treat a slow seller as information — drop it and double down on what moves.
