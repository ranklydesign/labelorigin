How much does it cost to start a skincare line?

The honest answer is a range, not a number — the path you choose changes the price more than anything else. This guide breaks a first launch into its real cost buckets, shows industry ranges for each, and ends with a worksheet for real quotes.

Updated 6 November 2026 • General educational information, not a quote. Every manufacturer prices differently — treat the ranges below as starting points for your own quotes.

How much does it cost to start a skincare line?

A first launch usually falls into one of two bands. A stock-formula private-label launch, one to three existing formulas with your branding, commonly lands between roughly $1,500 and $10,000 for the first production run. A custom-formulation launch commonly starts around $5,000 to $25,000+, because development, sampling, and testing come before a single unit is filled.

A first skincare launch usually falls into one of two bands. A stock-formula (private-label) launch — one to three existing formulas with your branding — commonly lands between roughly $1,500 and $10,000 for the first production run, depending on units and packaging. A custom-formulation launch — formulas developed to your specification — commonly starts around $5,000 to $25,000+ for the first run, because development, sampling rounds, and testing are added before a single unit is filled.

Those bands are industry-wide observations, not promises. A single stock product in a simple bottle can come in under the low end; a custom line with distinctive packaging and retail-ready cartons can pass the high end. The rest of this guide shows you why — so when quotes arrive, you can read them instead of guessing.

The number that matters most is units per product. Nearly every cost is priced per run or per unit, and minimums decide your floor. Decide how many units of each product you can realistically sell in three to six months — that number drives the whole budget.

The seven cost buckets

Every quote is some combination of these seven buckets. If a quote seems high or low, check which buckets it includes — comparison needs the same buckets on both sides.

1. Formula and development

With a stock formula, development is already done: you are paying for the product itself, usually as a per-unit fill price. With custom formulation, you pay for a chemist's time, raw materials for trial batches, and revision rounds. Industry-wide, custom development fees commonly run from a few thousand dollars for a straightforward product to five figures for complex formulas, usually billed separately from the production run.

2. Minimum order quantities (MOQs)

A minimum order quantity is the smallest run a manufacturer will do, almost always set per product (SKU), not per brand. Across the industry, stock-program minimums can be as low as the tens of units per product, while semi-custom and fully custom programs commonly start in the hundreds or thousands per SKU.

3. Packaging

Jars, bottles, pumps, caps, droppers, tubes, and outer cartons are usually billed per unit on top of the fill price, and each component can carry its own supplier minimum. Stock packaging in standard sizes is cheapest; custom-molded containers, metallic finishes, and airless pumps cost more and often require larger commitments.

4. Labels and printing

Labels include the printed label, application (many manufacturers apply labels in-house; some charge per unit), and required regulatory text such as ingredient lists and net contents. Digitally printed labels commonly run from roughly $0.10 to $1.00 per label at small volumes, falling as quantities rise. Outer cartons, inserts, and seals are separate line items. One quiet cost driver: every size, scent, or shade variant usually needs its own label run, so variants multiply label costs the same way they multiply MOQs.

5. Testing and compliance

Professional launches include testing, frequently billed separately from production. The common items are stability testing (does the product hold up over its shelf life), preservative-effectiveness testing (does it stay safe once opened and used), and, for some products and markets, safety assessments and claim-support testing. Industry-wide, a basic testing package for one formula commonly runs from several hundred to a few thousand dollars.

6. Brand and business setup

This bucket is yours, not the manufacturer's: business registration, trademark filing if you pursue one, barcode registration, product photography, and your sales channel. Photography surprises founders — clean shots for three SKUs commonly cost a few hundred dollars at the budget end and well over a thousand with a studio. None of this is manufacturing, but all of it is launch cost, and it is the bucket most often left out of first budgets entirely.

7. Launch marketing and samples

Samples serve two purposes: your own evaluation, and seeding product to reviewers, estheticians, or buyers. Sample programs vary enormously — flat fees per product, or near the per-unit production cost. Budget for at least two sample rounds on any custom formula: the first round almost never ships unchanged. For launch marketing itself, set a separate budget and keep it out of the production math; mixing the two is how founders discover they can afford to make a product but not to sell it.

Two rules make the worksheet work. Get every quote on the same basis — same products, units, and packaging — or the totals will mislead you. And treat any quote that will not itemize these buckets as a warning sign: itemizing is how a manufacturer shows you what you are paying for.

A worked example: three products

Suppose you launch three stock-formula products — cleanser, moisturizer, serum — at 250 units each, standard packaging. Using mid-range worksheet figures purely as illustration: fill at $4.00 per unit ($3,000), packaging at $1.50 ($1,125), labels at $0.40 ($300), testing included ($0), one sample round ($150), freight ($300), brand setup ($800). Illustrative total: about $5,675 before marketing.

Change one assumption and the total moves a lot. Drop to 100 units each and per-unit costs rise while freight and setup stay fixed, so the total falls only modestly while your cost per unit climbs. Switch to custom formulation and development fees add several thousand dollars before production starts. The lesson: units and path drive the budget, not the product idea. Run your own numbers with real quotes before committing to a launch size.

Five costs founders forget

  • Reorder minimums. Reorders usually carry the same minimums, so budget for the second run before the first sells out — stockouts cost more in lost sales than the reorder does.
  • Freight and warehousing. Finished goods are heavy, and shipping a pallet across the country is a real line item — as is storage from the day the shipment arrives.
  • Payment terms. Many manufacturers require a deposit (commonly around half), balance due before shipment — so cash is committed weeks or months before your first sale. Plan the gap.
  • Unsold inventory. Every unsold unit is money on a shelf with an expiry date. Fewer SKUs and smaller runs usually beat discounting dead stock later.
  • Regulatory responsibility. As brand owner, labeling and regulatory obligations generally sit with you, not the manufacturer: they make the product to specification; you make sure it can legally be sold as labeled. Budget time — and, if needed, professional advice — for that.

Getting an accurate quote

Manufacturers quote from specifics, not ideas. Send each one the same brief: product type and format, units per product, packaging preferences (or a request for stock options), target retail price, and timeline — plus anything that changes the work, like fragrance-free requirements or outer cartons.

Then ask the five questions that separate a complete quote from a partial one: Which tests are included, and what is billed separately? Is any development fee credited against the first order? What are the reorder minimums and payment terms? And what is the realistic lead time from approved samples to shipped goods? Clear answers show you how the relationship works after you pay, not just before.

Frequently Asked Questions

Skincare line costs
What is the cheapest way to start a skincare line?

Starting with stock (private-label) formulas is usually the cheapest route, because the formula already exists and you are paying mainly for product, packaging, and labels. Custom formulation costs more because development, sampling, and testing are added on top.

What is a minimum order quantity (MOQ)?

A minimum order quantity is the smallest number of units a manufacturer will produce in one run. It varies widely across the industry — from low double digits per product for some stock programs to thousands of units for fully custom work — and it is usually set per product (SKU), not per brand.

Do I need stability and safety testing before I sell?

Testing expectations depend on the product type, the claims you make, and where you sell. Stability testing, preservative-effectiveness testing, and safety assessments are standard parts of a professional launch. Ask any manufacturer what testing is included in their quote and what is billed separately.

How much should I budget for packaging and labels?

Packaging and labels are often 20 to 40 percent of the per-unit cost on a small first run. Jars, bottles, pumps, caps, boxes, and printed labels each carry their own minimums, so a distinctive pack can cost more than the formula inside it at low volumes.

Can I start a skincare line with $1,000?

It is possible only in narrow cases — for example, one stock product, a small run, and simple packaging — and only if the manufacturer's minimums allow it. Most founders planning two or three products should expect a first-launch budget in the low thousands at minimum, before marketing.

What should I send a manufacturer to get an accurate quote?

Send the product type, the number of units per product, your packaging preferences, your target retail price, and your timeline. A manufacturer cannot quote accurately from "I want a moisturizer" alone — units and packaging drive most of the price.
