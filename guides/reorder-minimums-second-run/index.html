Reorder Minimums and the Second Run

A successful first run creates a new problem that founders rarely plan for: the stock that is selling well will run out, and replacing it takes time, money, and a minimum order that cannot be wished away. The second run is not an afterthought. It is a cash-flow decision that needs to be made before the first run sells out.

The second run as a cash-flow problem

The second run is best understood as a cash-flow problem, not as a repeat of the excitement of the first launch. The first run is funded from savings, investment, or borrowing, before any sales revenue exists. The second run arrives at a different moment: sales have begun, revenue is arriving, and the temptation is to assume that revenue will fund the reorder smoothly and in good time. In practice, revenue arrives gradually, in step with sales, while a reorder needs a substantial sum committed at once, ahead of production, and ahead of delivery. The gap between gradual revenue and a lump-sum reorder is where founders get caught. They have sold well, they have revenue on paper, and they still cannot fund the reorder when it is needed, because the money has already been spent on the costs of selling, on the next round of marketing effort, or on the ordinary running of the business. Planning for the second run means planning for that gap, deliberately, from the start.

Reorder minimums usually match the first run

Reorders usually carry the same minimums as the first run. A minimum order quantity is set per product, and it reflects the smallest run a manufacturer can produce efficiently, taking account of setup, materials, filling, and handling. That logic does not disappear because a founder has already ordered once. The setup work, the material ordering, and the production scheduling that justified the minimum on the first run still apply on the second, and the manufacturer will usually apply the same floor. Some manufacturers may offer flexibility on a reorder, particularly where the formula, packaging, and labelling are unchanged and the relationship is established, but flexibility should never be assumed. It should be asked for, in writing, before it is relied on. A founder who assumes a smaller reorder will be accepted, without confirmation, risks a refusal at the moment stock is lowest and time is shortest.

Variants add the same complication on reorder that they add on the first run. Each variant — a different scent, size, or shade — is typically treated as its own product for minimum purposes, with its own label run and its own stock position. A range that looked manageable at launch can therefore become a complex reorder problem if variants proliferate without a clear view of which ones sell and which ones linger. Before ordering a second run, review variant-level sales honestly. A variant that has barely moved may not deserve a reorder at the full minimum, however much affection the founder has for it. A variant that has sold strongly may deserve a larger reorder, or earlier reordering, than the rest of the range. Those judgements are easier to make from sales records than from impressions, and they are easier to act on when the reorder minimums for each variant are known in advance. Confirm the minimums for every product and variant in writing, keep them with the sales records, and use them together when the reorder decision arrives.

When to reserve cash for the second run

Reserve cash for the second run before the first run sells out, with time allowed for production and delivery. That sentence is easy to write and hard to follow, because it asks a founder to commit funds to a future run while the current run is still selling and while other demands on cash feel more urgent. The discipline that makes it workable is a reorder point: a stock level, set in advance for each product, at which the reorder process starts. The reorder point should leave enough stock to sell while the reorder is produced and delivered, with a margin for slippage, and enough cash reserved to fund the reorder when it is placed. Setting the point is a judgement, based on the sales pace, the production and delivery time the manufacturer has quoted, and the founder's tolerance for running low. The exact level matters less than the existence of a level, agreed in advance, that triggers action without a fresh debate each time stock falls.

What a stockout costs

A stockout costs more in lost sales than the reorder does. That claim, from the cost guide, deserves unpacking, because the cost of being out of stock is broader than the sales missed on the days when nothing is available. A buyer who arrives ready to purchase and finds nothing available may buy a competitor's product instead, and may not return. A stockist who cannot obtain stock may fill the shelf with another brand and may be reluctant to clear space again when stock returns. A creator or esthetician who planned to feature a product may move on to something they can actually obtain, and the moment of attention that would have driven sales is lost. Each of those losses is hard to measure precisely, and each is easy to underestimate when the stockout is viewed only as a few days without sales. The true cost is the momentum lost, the relationships strained, and the buyers who quietly go elsewhere and do not explain why.

The per-unit bands, applied again

On reorder, the per-unit bands apply again, in the same way they applied on the first run. Fill, packaging, and labels are priced per unit, so the cost of a reorder is driven by the same per-unit figures, multiplied by the units in the reorder, with the same attention to variants and the same need to compare quotes on a like-for-like basis. The published bands are fill $1.50–$12.00 per unit, packaging $0.50–$5.00 per unit, and labels $0.10–$1.00 per label, each an estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote. Those bands do not become firmer or more precise because a reorder is involved. They remain starting points for judging quotes, and the actual reorder quote, from the actual manufacturer, for the actual products, units, and packaging, is the figure that should govern the reorder fund. A founder who assumes the reorder will cost exactly what the first run cost, without a fresh quote, risks a gap if pricing, packaging, or freight has changed in the meantime.

When you read reorder minimums in the skincare line cost guide, you will see the reorder point made briefly, as one of the costs founders forget: reorders usually carry the same minimums, so budget for the second run before the first sells out. This article expands that brief warning into a planning approach: confirm the minimums and terms in writing before the first run, set a reorder point for each product, build a reorder fund from sales from the start, and order in good time on the basis of a fresh itemised quote. The per-unit bands give the reorder fund its shape. The reorder point gives it its timing. Together, they turn the second run from a surprise into a routine, and they protect the momentum that the first run worked so hard to build.

A planning checklist for the second run

A short checklist, completed before the first run is ordered, makes reorder planning concrete and keeps it from being crowded out by launch excitement. Confirm the reorder minimums for each product and variant, in writing, and record them with the first-run quote. Confirm the pricing basis for reorders, including whether any charges from the first run will not recur, and whether any new charges will apply. Confirm the payment structure and the time from order to delivery, so the reorder fund and the reorder point can be set on real terms. Set a reorder point for each product, based on sales pace and delivery time, with a margin for slippage. Start a reorder fund from the beginning of selling, set aside from revenue, and review it alongside stock levels on a regular rhythm. And record the sales pace, product by product, so reorder decisions rest on evidence rather than on a general sense that things seem to be selling well.

  • Minimums confirmed. Reorder minimums for each product and variant, in writing, before the first run is ordered.
  • Pricing basis confirmed. Which first-run charges recur, which do not, and what a fresh itemised reorder quote will cover.
  • Reorder point set. A stock level for each product that triggers the reorder process with time to spare.
  • Reorder fund started. A share of revenue set aside from the start, reviewed alongside stock and sales pace.
  • Sales tracked by product. Pace and stock position recorded product by product, so fast movers are not hidden by averages.
  • Fresh quote before ordering. An itemised reorder quote, compared bucket by bucket with the first run, before funds are committed.

For the wider explanation of minimum order quantities, including the tiers that apply to stock, semi-custom, and custom programmes and the levers that can lower a first run, read low minimum order quantities, explained. That guide explains what minimums are and why they exist, while this article deals with the budgeting and timing decision that follows: when to reserve cash for the second run, and what happens if the decision is left until the shelves are empty. Keep the two together when planning a launch. A founder who understands minimums but has no reorder plan will still face the stockout gap. A founder with a reorder plan but no understanding of minimums will still be surprised by the size of the reorder required. Both pieces of understanding are needed, and both should be in place before the first run is ordered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Reorder minimums
Do reorder minimums match the first run minimums?

Reorders usually carry the same minimums as the first run, set per product. Confirm the reorder minimums in writing before you order the first run, so the second run can be planned on real terms.

When should I reserve cash for a reorder?

Reserve cash for the second run before the first run sells out, with time for production and delivery. Waiting until stock is gone leaves a gap with nothing to sell while the reorder is made and shipped.
