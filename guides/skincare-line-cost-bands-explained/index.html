What the two first-run cost bands include, and what they leave out

A band is only useful when you know what it assumes. This guide unpacks the two first-run bands from the cost guide, bucket by bucket, so you can see what each band is made of and what still sits outside it.

The question behind the bands

Founders usually arrive with a single question: what will a first launch cost? The cost guide answers with two bands rather than a single figure, because the path chosen changes the price more than anything else. The stock-formula band is $1,500–$10,000 (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote), and the custom-formulation band is $5,000–$25,000+ (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote). Those bands are general observations about how first runs tend to land across the industry, not promises about any one launch and not figures quoted by any one manufacturer. Each band describes a first production run on a stated basis, and each leaves certain launch costs outside its framing altogether.

That distinction matters because a band read as a complete launch total will mislead in both directions. A founder who treats the stock band as an all-in total may discover late in planning that separate lines still need funding. A founder who treats the custom band as a fixed price may over-commit to a scope the band never assumed. The useful question is therefore what each band assumes about formulas, units, packaging, and the work that happens before production. That is the question this guide answers, using only the two first-run cost bands in the skincare line cost guide as its source for figures.

A band is a framing device, not a quote. Use the two bands to sense-check whether a launch shape sits in stock territory or custom territory, then replace them with itemised quotes on a single, shared basis before making any commitment.

What the stock band assumes

The stock band describes a launch built on existing formulas. The formula already exists, so the founder is not funding the work of creating a formula from a brief. That single assumption shapes everything else inside the band. The launch is paying, in the main, for product, packaging, and labels on a first production run, with the formula itself already developed. The conversation with a manufacturer starts from products that already have a form and a format, rather than from an idea that still needs to become a workable formula.

The products inside the band

Inside the stock band, the products are existing formulas given the founder's branding. The founder's choices centre on which existing products to carry, how many units of each product to order, and how those products will be presented. The container, the closure, the outer carton where one is used, and the printed label all sit inside the production-run framing, and each variant of a product generally needs its own label run and its own handling. The band assumes a modest first lineup rather than an extensive range, because every additional product adds its own per-product handling to the run.

Units per product are the quiet assumption holding the stock band together. A band spanning $1,500–$10,000 (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote) can only hold together if the units ordered sit within a plausible first-run range for the products and packaging involved. Order far fewer units and the per-unit handling weighs more heavily; order far more and the total moves beyond the framing even though the path has not changed. The band does not state a unit count, and no unit count should be read into it. The point is structural: the stock band assumes units sized to what can realistically move in an opening period, rather than to a round number or the largest run imaginable.

What the custom band adds

The custom band describes a launch where formulas are developed to the founder's specification. Everything inside the stock framing is still present — product, packaging, and labels on a first production run — and additional work is added before production begins. That added work is the reason the custom band sits higher: development, sampling rounds, and testing are added on top of the production run, and each carries its own scope and billing. The custom band is $5,000–$25,000+ (estimate based on general industry patterns — not a quote), and the plus sign at the upper end is doing real work: a custom line with distinctive packaging and a fuller scope can pass the upper end, because the band describes a tendency, not a ceiling.

Development is the defining addition. A chemist's time, raw materials for trial batches, and revision rounds all sit inside the development line, and that line is usually billed separately from the production run. The separation matters for planning, because development money is committed before production money, and the founder needs to understand what the development fee covers, what counts as a revision within that fee, and what would trigger a further charge. Testing likewise needs to be pinned down in writing: which items are included in a quoted price, and which are billed separately, is a question the founder must ask rather than a detail the band can answer.

Custom adds work before production, not just cost during it. Development, sampling rounds, and testing are funded and completed before the production run they serve, so the custom band should be read as a longer runway as well as a higher range.

What sits outside both bands

Launch marketing and business setup

Launch marketing sits outside the production math as a matter of method in the cost guide: the guide keeps a separate budget for launch marketing and keeps it out of production totals, so founders can see clearly whether they can afford to make a product and whether they can afford to bring it to buyers. Mixing the two totals hides the real affordability question. The bands, as production-run framings, do not answer the marketing question, and no marketing figure should be read into either band. Business setup sits outside the manufacturer relationship in a similar way. Business registration, trademark filing where pursued, barcode registration, product photography, and the sales channel are the founder's own lines, not the manufacturer's, and they are the bucket most often left out of first budgets entirely.

Freight, storage, and the next run

Finished goods are physical and heavy, and need to travel and to be stored, and those realities generate lines that sit outside a simple production total. Freight to the founder, and storage from the day a shipment arrives, are real launch costs that the production band alone does not settle. A freight figure belongs early in planning, while the launch shape is still flexible, rather than late, when the shape is fixed and the freight line can only be accepted. The next run also sits outside the first-run bands. Reorders usually carry the same minimums as the first run, so the second run must be budgeted before the first run is gone, or the founder faces a gap in availability while a reorder is funded and produced. A launch planned only as far as the first delivery is a launch planned only halfway.

How to read a band without mistaking it for a quote

A band and a quote do different jobs, and the founder who confuses them will misread both. A band orients: it tells you which territory a launch shape sits in, and whether a quoted total looks broadly consistent with that territory or wildly out of line with it. A quote commits: it itemises what a particular manufacturer will supply, on what basis, and at what price. The band can prompt a question — why does this quote sit above the band, or below it? — but only the itemised quote can answer it. A quote below a band may simply include fewer lines; a quote above a band may include lines the band left outside its framing. Neither position is, on its own, a reason to accept or reject the quote.

Questions to ask before you rely on a band

Bands are starting points, and starting points earn their keep by generating good questions. Before relying on either band as a planning figure, a founder should be able to answer — or to ask a manufacturer — a short list of questions that pin the band framing to a real launch. Each one moves the conversation from the general framing of the band to the specific contents of a quote.

  • Which buckets does this quote include, and which are billed separately? Ask for itemisation across formula and development, fill, packaging, labels, testing, samples, freight, and brand setup, so no line hides inside a total.
  • What is the basis of comparison? Confirm the same products, units per product, and packaging across every quote, so totals can be compared honestly rather than impressionistically.
  • What sits outside the production total? Name launch marketing, business setup, freight, storage, and the next run explicitly, and confirm each has its own budget and timing.
  • How are testing and sampling treated? Ask which testing items are included and which are billed separately, and how sample rounds are charged, before assuming the band has settled either question.
  • What changes the position within the band? Ask which choices — units, packaging, variants, or development scope — would move the total toward the upper end, so scope decisions are made deliberately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Skincare launch cost bands
Does the stock band include everything a launch needs?

No. The stock band frames the first production run for existing formulas. Costs that sit outside that framing, such as launch marketing kept separate from production math, still need their own budget.

Why is the custom band higher than the stock band?

The custom band adds work that the stock band does not need: formula development, sampling rounds, and testing that happen before production. Those added lines are what move the custom band above the stock band.
