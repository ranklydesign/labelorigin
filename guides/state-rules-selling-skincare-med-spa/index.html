Selling skincare from your treatment room is legal in every state we checked — but the paperwork is two separate registrations from two separate agencies, and the names change at every border. What are the state rules for selling skincare in a med spa?

In the 50 states and the District of Columbia verified here, selling retail skincare inside a licensed salon needs two things: the salon or shop license — from the cosmetology board in 49 of them, from the local health district in Connecticut — and a sales-tax permit from the revenue department. Utah is the exception on the salon side: it has no state-level salon license at all. No verified state requires a separate retail license. Medical services in a med spa are a separate question, governed by the medical board.

That is the short version of a table that took a day of reading official pages to build — because the answer is never in one place. The cosmetology board licenses the room and the people in it. The revenue department licenses the sale. Neither agency's site mentions the other's requirement, and neither mentions a retail-specific license, because in the states we verified there is not one. The table below names both agencies for each state, with a link to each official page, so you can confirm both halves yourself before you open a shelf. Our 2026 med spa retail report covers the market side — how many spas retail, what they earn, and what the shelf is worth — while this page covers the permission side.

Two registrations, two agencies. The salon license comes from the cosmetology board; the sales-tax permit comes from the revenue department. No verified state adds a third, retail-specific license from the cosmetology board — but the medical side of a med spa is a separate regulator, covered below.

How to use this table

Find your state, read across the row, then click the two links in the Source column and confirm both requirements on the official pages before you act. Three steps, in order:

  • Licensing body: the agency that licenses the salon or shop, and the name of the license it issues. This is the permission to operate the room.
  • Separate retail license? whether the cosmetology board requires an additional license just to sell retail products inside that salon. In every verified state the answer is either no, or the board's site does not state one.
  • Sales-tax permit? the permit that authorizes you to collect sales tax on retail sales, and the agency that issues it. Register before your first taxable sale — every verified state requires it in advance.

If your state is in the not-yet-verified list, the table has nothing to say about it yet — that is deliberate. A state appears here only after both halves have been checked against the official page. Rules change, and a board page can move; the Source links are there so you can verify the current wording yourself, on the day you act.

Five rows deserve a footnote. California is the only verified state where the official page enumerates the full requirement list — the Governor’s business guide for barber shops and beauty salons names the establishment license and the seller’s permit and nothing else — so it is the only row that says “No” outright. Illinois is the only verified state where the regulator has published the med-spa split in writing: a joint IDFPR and Department of Public Health memo, updated 30 October 2025, states that a salon registration cannot be used to operate a med spa, and that medical procedures fall within physician scope. Oregon, Alaska, Delaware, Montana, and New Hampshire are the verified states with no statewide sales tax — their rows say so plainly, with the sales-tax column pointing to the official page. Hawaii has no sales tax either, but it is not in that group: Hawaii levies a general excise tax on businesses instead, and a GET license is required — its row says so plainly. New Mexico is the same shape: no traditional sales tax, but a gross receipts tax (GRT) on businesses instead — its row says so plainly. Connecticut is the first verified state with no state-level shop license — the Department of Public Health licenses the practitioners, and the local health district licenses the premises, as the Chesprocott Health District ordinance shows. Utah is the second: the Cosmetology Act licenses practitioners and schools only, with no salon or shop license at all — Utah Code §58-11a-301.

Med spas vs. estheticians: two regulators, not one

The table covers the cosmetology side: the salon, the shop, the esthetician. Every verified state licenses that side through its cosmetology board, whatever the board is called — a standalone board in California, North Carolina, and Nevada; a division of the licensing department in Texas, Illinois, and Washington; a board inside the Department of State in New York and Pennsylvania. The license names vary — establishment license, salon license, shop license, certificate of registration — but the shape is the same: the premises are licensed, inspected, and tied to a location. Connecticut is the exception on the premises side: the state licenses the practitioners through the Department of Public Health, and the salon premises are licensed by the local health district. Utah is the second exception: the state licenses the practitioners and the schools, but no salon or shop license exists at all.

A med spa has a second half the table does not cover. Anything medical — injectables, lasers, medium-depth peels, prescription-strength treatments — is practiced under a physician's license and regulated by the state medical board, not the cosmetology board. Illinois is the verified example: the IDFPR and Department of Public Health memo states that a salon registration cannot be used to operate a med spa. The retail question and the medical question are separate permissions from separate regulators, and a spa that does both needs both. The MoCRA guide for spas selling skincare covers a third layer — the federal product rules on labeling and safety — which applies in every state on top of the state licenses in this table. The med-spa private-label page and the estheticians' private-label page describe how the own-label side works once the permissions are in place.

Sales tax: the permit names change, the rule does not

Every verified state that has a sales tax requires the same thing: register with the revenue department and get the permit before your first taxable sale. The permit's name is the only thing that changes — seller's permit in California, Certificate of Authority in New York and New Jersey, vendor's license in Ohio, Standard Retail License in Colorado, Sales Tax Registration Certificate in Massachusetts. The license is separate from the salon license, it comes from a different agency, and it must be displayed at the place of business in most of the states we checked. Hawaii is the outlier on the tax side: it has no sales tax, but it levies a general excise tax (GET) instead, and a GET license is required.

The same registration usually covers buying inventory for resale without paying sales tax at purchase, using the state's resale certificate — Washington's reseller permit and Tennessee's Certificate of Resale are the named examples in the official pages we read. That is a second reason to register before the first sale rather than after: the permit is what lets you buy stock tax-free as well as sell it taxed. Frequently Asked Questions

State rules for selling skincare
Do I need a separate license to sell retail skincare in my spa?

In the 50 states and the District of Columbia verified here, no. Every verified jurisdiction requires the salon or shop license and a sales-tax permit from the revenue department — but no board we checked issues a separate license just for selling retail products inside a licensed salon. The salon license comes from the cosmetology board in 49 of them; in Connecticut the premises are licensed by the local health district while the state licenses the practitioners, and Utah has no state-level salon license at all — the Cosmetology Act licenses practitioners and schools only. California's official business guide is the clearest case: it lists only the establishment license and a seller's permit. If your state is not in the table yet, check with your state board before you assume the same.

Who licenses a med spa — the cosmetology board or the medical board?

Both can apply, to different halves of the business. The cosmetology board licenses the salon and the estheticians who work in it — that is the side this table covers. Medical services such as injectables and lasers fall under the physician's license and the state medical board. Illinois states the split plainly: a salon registration issued by its cosmetology regulator cannot be used to operate a med spa.

Do I need a sales-tax permit to sell skincare in my spa?

Yes, in every verified state that has a sales tax. Each state calls the permit something different — a seller's permit in California, a Certificate of Authority in New York and New Jersey, a vendor's license in Ohio — and each requires you to register before your first taxable sale. The permit is separate from your salon license, and it comes from the revenue department, not the cosmetology board.

What if my state is not in the table?

It is in the not-yet-verified list below the table, with Puerto Rico. We only publish a row once we have checked both halves — the licensing body and the sales-tax permit — against the official state page. Until then we list the state name only and make no claim about its rules. Check your state board's site directly in the meantime.
