Unsold inventory and expiry: the cost of over-ordering

Every unsold unit is money on a shelf with an expiry date. This guide explains why ordering too much is one of the quietest ways a first launch loses money, and how calmer run sizing protects cash, space, and the life of the stock itself.

What unsold stock really is

Unsold stock looks harmless. It sits in boxes, neatly labelled, ready to sell, and it feels like an asset because it once cost money and still holds value in principle. In practice, unsold skincare is cash that has changed form. The money is no longer in your account, where it could fund a reorder or the everyday running of the business. It is in jars and bottles, waiting for buyers who may never arrive in the numbers you hoped for. Cash is flexible and stock is not: cash can be redirected in a day, while stock can only be sold one unit at a time, to people who want that exact product before its saleable life runs out.

The expiry date is what makes skincare different from many other kinds of stock. A spare part can sit on a shelf for years and still do its job, but a skincare product has a life written into it from the day it is made. As that life runs down, the window for selling it narrows and the pressure to move it grows. Decisions that should be calm — how to present a product, who to approach, what to try next — start to feel urgent, because every passing week shortens the time left to sell what is already made. That urgency rarely leads to good judgement. It leads to hurried choices and heavy discounting, even when the product itself may be sound and the only real problem is the size of the first run. Keeping that distinction in mind is the starting point for sizing a first run with care, and it is the thread that runs through unsold inventory risk in the skincare line cost guide, where unsold inventory sits among the costs founders most often forget.

Why ordering more feels safer than it is

Ordering more feels like prudence. A founder imagines a buyer asking for stock and having to say no, and the embarrassment of that moment feels worse than the quiet cost of extra boxes. The fear of running out is vivid and immediate, while the cost of over-ordering is slow and abstract, spread over months of storage and a gradually shortening saleable life. Judgement weighs the vivid risk more heavily, so founders lean toward the larger run and tell themselves that extra stock is only a problem if sales are slow, and that sales will surely be strong enough. That reasoning treats demand as something to hope for rather than something to test, and it puts the whole weight of the launch on a guess made before a single unit has been sold.

Variety makes the pull stronger. A founder does not just order more of one product; they add another scent, another size, or another shade, because each variant feels like another chance to catch a buyer. Each variant, though, is its own run, with its own label, its own packaging, and its own pile of stock to sell through. A launch that began as a focused idea can quietly become a warehouse of small piles, none of which is large enough to matter, and the founder ends up spreading attention across many products instead of learning deeply about one or two. There is also a subtler pressure: the feeling that a small run is not a serious launch. Established brands built their ranges over time, learning what sold before committing to more. A modest run that sells out teaches far more, and leaves far more cash intact, than a generous run that lingers.

More than the purchase cost

The purchase cost is only the first part of what unsold stock costs. Storage begins the day the shipment arrives: finished goods are heavy, they take up real space, and that space has to come from somewhere. Storage is not only rent. It is handling, checking, keeping stock organised, and keeping track of which batch is oldest and needs to move first. None of that work sells a single unit, but all of it has to be done, and it grows with the size of the pile. A founder who imagined spending their time finding buyers can find instead that much of the week goes to managing boxes, while the work that actually grows a brand — talking to buyers, listening to feedback, improving the offer — gets crowded out.

Fewer products, smaller runs

The counterweight to over-ordering is judgement grounded in how much you can realistically sell. Start from sell-through, not from ambition: ask how many units of each product you could sell, through the channels you actually have, in the time the stock will stay fresh. That is a humbling question, because the honest answer is usually smaller than the hopeful one. It forces a founder to name real buyers and real routes to market, rather than relying on a general belief that people will want the product. A run sized to that honest answer may look modest, but it is the run most likely to sell through, to generate useful feedback, and to leave cash available for the next step.

Smaller runs do carry a trade-off, and it is worth stating plainly. The per-unit bands for fill, packaging, and labels are bands, not fixed prices, and the cost of each unit generally sits higher when a run is smaller, because fixed work is spread across fewer units. But the comparison that matters is not between the cost of each unit on a small run and the cost of each unit on a large run. It is between the total cash committed and the total cash recovered. A small run with a higher cost per unit that sells through can leave a founder better off than a large run with a lower cost per unit that leaves much of its stock unsold. The saving on each unit only helps if the unit actually sells. Fewer products help for the same reason: each product is a separate pile of stock to sell through, and a focused range is easier to explain, easier to sell, and easier to learn from. The guide to low minimum order quantities explained sets out how minimums work and how a first run can be kept modest; read it alongside this guide when weighing how many products to launch.

All of the figures behind these judgements are estimates based on general industry patterns — not a quote. No figure in this guide should be treated as a price from any manufacturer. The per-unit bands referenced here are useful for understanding the direction of the trade-off — smaller runs, higher cost per unit — and for comparing quotes on the same basis. They are not a substitute for real quotes, and they do not tell you what your run will cost. What they do tell you is how to think: size the run to realistic sell-through first, understand the unit-cost trade-off second, and never let a lower cost per unit talk you into stock you have no clear route to sell.

Signs you may be ordering too much

The clearest warning sign is the absence of evidence. If you cannot name the buyers, conversations, or channels that will move the stock, the run is being sized on hope, and hope is not a sales channel. A founder with a list of interested buyers or a committed route to market has something to size against. A founder with only a general belief that the product will sell has no anchor, and the run size becomes a guess dressed up as a plan. That does not mean a first-time founder cannot launch. It means a first-time founder should launch small, treat the first run as a test, and let the results set the size of the second run. Evidence earned cheaply is worth more than confidence bought expensively.

What to do with stock that is not moving

If stock is already sitting unsold, the first step is an honest review, not a hurried price cut. Look at each product separately: which ones moved, even slowly, and which ones barely moved at all? Who bought, through which channel, and what did buyers actually say? That review separates products with a slow start from products with no real demand, and the two need different responses. A slow starter may need better presentation, a clearer audience, or more time in front of the right buyers. A product with no demand needs a different decision, and prolonging its life with repeated discounting only delays that decision while the saleable life runs down.

A calmer way to size a first run

Calmer sizing begins before any quote is requested. Write down, for each product, the buyers you can actually reach and the route by which you will reach them, in specific terms. If the list is short, the run should be short. That is not a judgement on the product; it is a judgement on the evidence, and evidence can be grown. A first run that sells through a short list of real buyers creates the evidence for a larger second run, while a first run that overshoots a short list creates stock, and stock creates pressure. Build in a review point as well: decide, before the stock arrives, when you will review sell-through and what you will do at that review, so the decision is made in advance, when judgement is calm, rather than in the moment, when the calendar is pressing.

Finally, treat the first run as the start of a conversation with the market, not as the final word on your brand. The products that sell, the buyers who return, and the feedback that repeats are all information. A founder who listens to that information — adding a variant because buyers asked for it, reordering a product because it sold through, dropping a product because it did not — builds a range grounded in demand. The difference between that founder and one who builds a warehouse is rarely talent or product quality. It is usually run size, and the willingness to let the first run be small enough to learn from. Order for the buyers you have, learn from what they do, and let the second run be the one that grows.

Frequently Asked Questions

Skincare line costs
What happens to skincare units I do not sell?

Every unsold unit is money on a shelf with an expiry date. As time passes, the saleable life of the stock runs down, the cash tied up in it stays out of reach, and the space it occupies cannot be used for anything else. Ordering only what you can realistically sell protects both cash and shelf life.

Is ordering a larger run always cheaper in the end?

Not when much of the run goes unsold. A larger run can lower the cost of each unit, but that saving disappears if the extra units sit unsold until they pass their saleable life. A smaller run that sells through usually leaves a founder in a stronger position than a larger run that leaves dead stock behind.
